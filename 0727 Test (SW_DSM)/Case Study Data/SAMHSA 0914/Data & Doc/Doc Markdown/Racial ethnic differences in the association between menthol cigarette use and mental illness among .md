Augustus M. White ${ }^{\mathrm{a}, \mathrm{b}}$ (D, Andrew J. Barnes ${ }^{\mathrm{a}, \mathrm{b}}$ (D) and William Garner ${ }^{\mathrm{c}}$ (D)<br>${ }^{\mathrm{a}}$ Department of Health Behavior and Policy, Virginia Commonwealth University, Richmond, Virginia, USA; ${ }^{\text {b }}$ Center for the Study of Tobacco Products, Virginia Commonwealth University, Richmond, Virginia, USA; ${ }^{\text {c }}$ Department of Public Health, University of North Texas at Dallas, Dallas, Texas, USA


#### Abstract

Data from the National Survey on Drug Use and Health (20122018) were used to characterize the association between menthol cigarette use and indicators of Any (AMI) and Serious (SMI) Mental Illness among adults who smoke in the United States. In general, people who smoke menthol cigarettes were more likely to have $\mathrm{AMI}(\mathrm{aOR}=1.123$ [1.063-1.194]) than people who smoke non-menthol cigarettes, but not SMI (aOR $=1.065$ [0.966-1.175]). However, among non-Hispanic African American/ Black people who smoke, those that used menthol cigarettes had lower adjusted odds of both AMI (aOR $=0.740$ [0.572$0.958]$ ) and SMI (aOR $=0.592$ [0.390-0.899]) than their counterparts who used non-menthol cigarettes. Results suggest there may be race/ethnicity-specific drivers of the association between menthol cigarette use and mental illness.


## KEYWORDS

Menthol cigarettes; mental illness; race/ethnicity; national survey on drug use and health; tobacco use

## Introduction

Adults with a mental illness are $70 \%$ more likely to smoke cigarettes than adults in the general population (Ziedonis et al., 2008). This phenomenon has traditionally been explained by the "self-medication model" (Lerman et al., 1996), which argues that those with mental illness use cigarettes to cope with the symptoms of their illness and the side effects of their medications. However, recent work has argued that the constituents of cigarettes and smoking behaviors themselves may also be a cause of mental illness (Gage \& Munafò, 2015; Gurillo et al., 2015; Kendler et al., 1993; Wootton et al., 2020).

Consistent with both perspectives, there is concern regarding whether use of menthol cigarettes might be associated with an even greater incidence of mental illness among people who smoke (Brunette et al., 2019;

[^0]Cohn et al., 2016; Hickman et al., 2014; Young-Wolff et al., 2015). Menthol is a cigarette additive that produces a "minty, cooling, and soothing" sensation in the airways of the user-masking the natural harshness of tobacco (Ahijevych \& Garrett, 2010)-and is currently the only characterizing flavor of cigarette available in the United States (US). People who smoke menthol cigarettes have been shown to be more dependent upon nicotine and have less success when trying to quit smoking than people who smoke non-menthol cigarettes (Bover et al., 2008; P. Fagan et al., 2010; Villanti et al., 2017). While $35 \%$ of US people who smoke use menthol cigarettes, nearly $90 \%$ of African American/Black people who smoke do (Weinberger et al., 2019) as do majorities of Hispanic, youth, and young adult people who smoke (Nonnemaker et al., 2019). Even more troublingly, the heightened associations between menthol cigarette use (relative to non-menthol cigarette use) and subjective reward (Cohn et al., 2022), smoking initiation (D'Silva, Cohn, Johnson, \& Villanti, 2018), and difficulty quitting (Smith et al., 2020) appear strongest among African American/Black individuals (Stahre et al., 2010). These findings are perhaps unsurprising given the tobacco industry's decades-worth of multi-media advertising campaigns which have targeted racial/ethnic minority groups, as well as the strategic placement of mentholated products in these communities (Gardiner, 2004; Garner et al., 2020; Kong et al., 2020). This "menthol disparity" is likely part of the reason why racial/ethnic minoritized individuals suffer from disproportionately high rates of tobacco-induced morbidity and mortality in the US (Schoenborn et al., 2013).

Prior research has suggested that people who smoke with a serious mental illness are more likely to use menthol cigarettes than non-menthol cigarettes (Young-Wolff et al., 2015), that the prevalence of menthol cigarette smoking is higher among those with severe psychological distress (Brunette et al., 2019; Hickman et al., 2014; J. M. Williams et al., 2007), and that people who smoke menthol cigarettes report worse overall mental health than people who smoke non-menthol cigarettes (Hooper et al., 2011; Mendiondo et al., 2010). Similarly, previous investigators have concluded that "past 30 -day menthol tobacco use was uniquely associated with greater odds of both depression and anxiety" among a sample of young adults (Cohn et al., 2016). We also know that the addition of menthol to cigarettes alters smoking behaviors (e.g., facilitates longer puffs) and menthol is a neurologically active compound that can alter nicotine's activity, absorption, and metabolism (Pebbles Fagan et al., 2016; Henderson et al., 2017; Wickham, 2020). Through these mechanisms, menthol influences the levels of neurotransmitters that are implicated in the development of mental illness (e.g., dopamine, serotonin, GABA, and acetylcholine) (Benowitz, 2008; Picciotto \& Corrigall, 2002; Wang et al., 2019).

Concerns regarding the adverse effects of menthol in cigarettes are made even more relevant by the US Food and Drug Administration's (FDA) intent to ban menthol as a characterizing flavor in cigarettes (US Food \& Drug Administration, 2022). To inform this regulatory activity and maximizes its potential to promote health equity, FDA has requested additional evidence on both the expected and possible unanticipated consequences of a menthol ban with respect to the health of people who smoke (Tobacco Product Standard for Menthol in Cigarettes, 2022). Currently, we do not have sufficient evidence regarding whether menthol might enhance the association between smoking and mental illness among people who smoke. Few investigations have used recent, large-scale, and nationally-representative surveys to evaluate the association between menthol cigarette use and mental illness among adults who smoke. Moreover, given the primacy of race/ethnicity in conversations regarding menthol cigarettes, it is important to determine if the association between menthol cigarette use and mental illness differs by race/ethnicity. Such information would constitute a novel contribution to the literature and have important implications for the equitable design of tobacco regulatory policies currently under consideration (e.g., FDA's menthol cigarette ban). Further research characterizing the association between menthol cigarette use and mental illness, as well as moderators/mediators of that relationship, is thus merited.

The primary objective of these analyses is to quantify the association between the use of menthol cigarettes and validated indicators of Any Mental Illness (AMI) and Serious Mental Illness (SMI) among US adults who smoke, using data from 7 -years of the National Survey on Drug Use and Health (NSDUH). This work also tests for differences in the association between menthol cigarette use and indicators of mental illness among people who smoke, across racial/ethnic groups. This latter aim was established out of recognition that one's racial/ethnic background impacts not only tobacco use decisions and mental health, but the levels of many confounders including income (Bound et al., 2003; Fryer et al., 2013; Sykes \& Maroto, 2016), educational attainment (Levy et al., 2016; Quintana \& Mahgoub, 2016), and health status (Colen et al., 2018; Egede, 2006; Geronimus et al., 2006). These differences are not manifested by inherent biological differences between racial/ethnic groups, but rather the confluence of cultural, societal, political, environmental, and economic stressors that give meaning to the construction of racial/ethnic identities (Link \& Phelan, 1995; D. R. Williams \& Sternthal, 2010). Consequently, we would not interpret any racial/ethnic differences identified here to be suggestive of biological differences between groups, but rather crude proxies for social factors that give meaning to racial/ethnic identities (Link \& Phelan, 1995), drive tobacco use decisions (Gardiner, 2004), and may impact mental
health outcomes (Cummings \& Druss, 2011). It is hypothesized that the adjusted odds of AMI and SMI were higher for people who smoke menthol cigarettes, relative to people who smoke non-menthol cigarettes, across all racial/ethnic groups studied.

## Materials and methods

## Data source and study population

These analyses utilize publicly-available data from the Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration's (SAMHSA) National Survey on Drug Use and Health (NSDUH), collected from 2012 through 2018 (Substance Abuse \& Mental Health Data Archives, 2022). NSDUH is a nationally-representative survey of the civilian, non-institutionalized population of the US ages 12 and older. Data for the NSDUH is collected through annual, cross-sectional personal household interviews in which household addresses are selected using stratified, multi-stage, area probability sampling in all 50 states and the District of Columbia. The NSDUH collects data on $\sim 70,000$ households each year and has a response rate of $\sim 50 \%$. To account for nonresponse bias, data are weighted by 16 components, including demographic characteristics from 2010 Census population estimates.

The study sample was limited to those individuals who had smoked at least 1 cigarette in the 30 days preceding data collection and had smoked at least 100 cigarettes in their lifetime (Pomerleau et al., 2004). To be included in these analyses, respondents also had to be 18 to 64 years old at the time of data collection. After applying these inclusion criteria, the overall unweighted sample size included $\mathrm{N}=61,250$ (weighted $\mathrm{N}=42,649,828$ ) adults who smoke.

## Outcomes

There are two main outcome measures considered in these analyses: presence/absence of past-year AMI and presence/absence of past-year SMI. The NSDUH asked respondents to complete the Kessler-6 (K6) Psychological Distress Scale (Kessler et al., 2002) and the World Health Organization Disability Assessment Schedule (WHODAS) (Konecky et al., 2014), and to answer questions regarding past-year suicidal ideations, major depressive episodes, and age. Responses to these questions were then combined using an algorithm (defined by NSDUH) to determine the probability that a respondent had past-year AMI and SMI which were then converted to a binary outcome (yes/no) based on a pre-specified threshold value, selected to equalize the number of false-positives and false-negatives.

## Independent variable

The main independent variable in these analyses was current menthol cigarette use. Respondents were defined as "people who smoke menthol cigarettes" if they reported that the cigarettes they used most often in the past 30 days were menthol flavored. All other people who smoke were classified as "people who smoke non-menthol cigarettes."

## Covariates

Analyses included an array of covariates known to be associated with menthol cigarette use and mental illness. Specifically, regressions controlled for self-reported race/ethnicity, survey year, the average number of cigarettes a respondent smoked per day on days they smoked, gender, age, income, educational attainment, overall self-perception of health, whether or not the respondent was covered by any health insurance, and if the respondent self-reported past-year "abuse" of alcohol, cocaine, marijuana, or heroin.

Additionally, though NSDUH defines seven categories for race/ethnicity, we collapsed the race/ethnicity variable to four groups: non-Hispanic White (NH-W), non-Hispanic African American/Black (NH-AA), Hispanic, and "other." The "other" race/ethnicity category, which represents roughly $6 \%$ of our sample, included those identifying as non-Hispanic Native American/ Alaska Native, non-Hispanic Native Hawaiian/other Pacific Islander, non-Hispanic Asian, or non-Hispanic multi-racial. We did not take the decision to collapse these distinct racial-ethnic groups lightly, but due to sample size limitations felt this was the soundest approach based on the availability of data. Main findings were not sensitive to our decision to collapse race/ethnicity to four groups.

## Analytical methods

Survey data were weighted to account for the complex survey design of the NSDUH and to produce nationally-representative estimates of the US civilian, non-institutionalized population from 2012-2018. The NSDUH public-use data files include variables to obtain weighted estimates and corrected standard errors via the Taylor-series linearization approach. Unadjusted differences in AMI/SMI and our set of covariates between people who smoke menthol cigarettes and people who smoke non-menthol cigarettes were explored using F-tests. Adjusted associations between menthol cigarette use and AMI/SMI were evaluated using two separate logistic regressions, one for each mental illness outcome.

Importantly, because there are known racial/ethnic differences in menthol cigarette use, AMI/SMI prevalence, and many of the included covariates, the pooled regression analyses were repeated but stratified by race/ ethnicity, per recommendations from the literature (Dennis et al., 2021; Shi et al., 2005). This was an important analytic step as it acknowledged that race/ethnicity impacts the associations between demographic, health, and other tobacco/drug use confounders used in these analyses and mental illness, as well as menthol cigarette use. In this way, stratification permitted race/ethnicity to interact with all other covariates allowing for the distribution of confounders to vary across each racial/ethnic group of people who smoke. Post-estimation adjusted Wald Chi-Squared analyses tested for homogeneity in the estimates of the association between menthol cigarette use and AMI/SMI between the race/ethnicity stratified regressions (Kaufman \& MacLehose, 2013).

Stata SE/17 (College Station, TX) was used to conduct all analyses. Pre-specified significance level was set to 0.05 for two-sided hypothesis tests.

## Results

## Sample characteristics

The final weighted dataset represented $42,649,828$ people who smoked from the years 2012 to 2018 (Table 1). Of these, $28.5 \%$ [95\% Confidence Interval: 28.0-29.1\%] were designated as having past-year AMI and $8.7 \%$ [8.3-9.0\%] were determined to have past-year SMI. In terms of menthol cigarette use, $38.5 \%$ [37.8-39.3\%] reported using menthol cigarettes as their most commonly used cigarette in the past 30 days. Full demographic information can be found in Table 1.

## Unadjusted differences in mental illness outcomes and covariates

Unadjusted differences in mental health outcomes suggested a significantly higher proportion of people who smoke menthol cigarettes ( $29.7 \%$ ) were designated as having AMI than were people who smoke non-menthol cigarettes ( $27.9 \%$ ) ( $\mathrm{F}=13.76, p=0.0003$; Table 2 and Supplementary Table A). In contrast, the prevalence of SMI was found to be similar between people who smoke menthol cigarettes and people who smoke non-menthol cigarettes ( $8.7 \%$ vs. $8.6 \%$, respectively; $\mathrm{F}=0.10, p=0.7560$ ).

## Pooled adjusted association in mental illness outcomes and menthol cigarette use

Compared to people who smoke non-menthol cigarettes, the odds of experiencing past-year AMI were significantly higher ( $\sim 13 \%$ ) for people who smoke menthol cigarettes after accounting for demographic, perceived health, and other tobacco and drug use characteristics (adj. OR $=1.127$ [1.063-1.194]) (Table 3). In contrast, the odds of past-year SMI for people who smoke menthol cigarettes did not significantly differ from those of people who smoke non-menthol cigarettes (adj. OR $=1.065$ [0.966-1.175]).

There were significantly increased odds for experiencing past-year AMI and SMI among respondents who smoked more cigarettes per day, controlling for whether those cigarettes were mentholated ( $\mathrm{ps}<0.05$ ). NH-AA people who smoke were also found to have significantly lower overall odds of AMI (adj. OR $=0.607$ [0.556-0.663]) and SMI (adj. OR $=0.516$ [0.438-0.608]) than NH-W people who smoke, as were Hispanic people who smoke (AMI: adj. OR $=0.794$ [0.721-0.875]; SMI: adj. OR $=0.776$ [0.673-0.894]).

## Sensitivity analyses

Results described above were not sensitive to alternative specifications as probit or linear probability models. Additionally, the main results were robust to propensity score matching (using people who smoke menthol cigarettes as the "treated" group) in both the pooled and race/ethnici-ty-stratified analyses. Propensity score matching was used to assess whether differences in mental health outcomes between people who smoke menthol versus non-menthol cigarettes could be attributed to differences in the observable characteristics related to using a menthol or non-menthol cig-arette-which they were not. Notably, when the race/ethnicity covariate was dropped from pooled regressions for sensitivity analyses, the adjusted odds of AMI and SMI no longer differed by menthol status (Supplementary

Table D). The observation that omitting race/ethnicity from pooled regressions biases the association between menthol cigarette (vs non-menthol cigarette) usage and mental illness toward the null suggests that race/ ethnicity-itself a proxy for other social constructs - operates as a confounder of the association between menthol cigarette use and indicators of mental illness among people who smoke. This finding affirms the need to characterize race/ethnicity specific associations between menthol usage and indicators of mental illness (McNamee, 2005).

## Discussion

These results support the conclusion that being a menthol smoker (compared to a non-menthol smoker) is positively associated with experiencing symptoms of mental illness, in general. While this trend held true for NH-W and Hispanic people who smoke, notably, when these models were stratified by race/ethnicity, NH-AA people who smoke menthol cigarettes were found to have lower odds of both AMI and SMI than NH-AA people who smoke non-menthol cigarettes.

Taken together, these results indicate that menthol cigarette use is associated with indicators of co-occurring mental illness among people who smoke. However, whether menthol cigarette usage (relative to non-menthol cigarette usage) may play a role in the development of mental illness or simply may be sought after by people who smoke with a preexisting mental illness remains unclear. These findings are largely consistent with the literature (Brunette et al., 2019; Cohn et al., 2016; Hickman et al., 2014; Hooper et al., 2011; Mendiondo et al., 2010; J. M. Williams et al., 2007; Young-Wolff et al., 2015), but our analyses contrast slightly with prior work by failing to demonstrate a statistically significant association between SMI and menthol cigarette use. This could be because much of the prior research utilized data that pre-dated the 2009 ban on characterizing flavors in cigarettes other than menthol (Hickman et al., 2014; Hooper et al., 2011; Mendiondo et al., 2010) or the 2012 update to NSDUH's methodology for calculating the probability of AMI/SMI (Hickman et al., 2014). Additionally, our study looked at adult people who smoke aged 18-64, while other studies restricted analyses to young adults who are more likely to experience mental illness (Brunette et al., 2019; Cohn et al., 2016).

Our results also suggest that the use of menthol cigarettes may not be uniformly associated with mental illness among people who smoke. Notably, the population-level associations between the use of menthol cigarettes and past-year mental illness may be an example of Simpson's Paradox (Simpson, 1951). The differences in the pooled and stratified results of the analyses presented here are particularly instructive.

Estimates for NH-AA people who smoke were shown to differ from both the pooled regression results and the race/ethnicity-stratified estimates for Hispanic and NH-W people who smoke-findings, that to our knowledge, contribute novel knowledge to the extant literature. These differences highlight the importance of performing analyses stratified by race/ethnicity when possible, as factors related to structural racism and sociocultural differences can influence relationships of interest in complex ways (Link \& Phelan, 1995; Shi et al., 2005; D. R. Williams \& Sternthal, 2010). Such a procedure stands in contrast to simply adjusting for race/ethnicity or interacting race/ethnicity with the main predictor variable in a regression, as race/ethnicity can meaningfully impact the magnitude and direction of the associations of and between covariates. As was demonstrated in the pooled analyses, using race/ethnicity as an independent variable-without considering how race/ethnicity modifies menthol cigarette use and confounders of menthol use and mental health in the empirical model-masked significant heterogeneity in associations between menthol use and mental illness across racial/ethnic groups. Adoption of an "intersectional" approach to understanding associations between menthol cigarette usage, race/ethnicity, and mental illness is thus merited (Tan et al., 2013). Stratification permits the association of sociodemographic factors with mental health to vary for racialized groups-as they do in the real world due to interpersonal, institutional, and systematic racism-rather than holding them artificially constant across the entire population.

Results presented here inform understanding of the public health impact of the continued availability of menthol cigarettes. Specifically, our results suggest that use of menthol cigarettes is associated with indicators of co-occurring mental illness (relative to smoking of non-menthol cigarettes) at the population level. However, this association is not uniform across people who smoke of different racial/ethnic identities- a revelation that should prompt further research into the sociodemographic factors driving menthol use and mental illness in different communities. Importantly, because of the cross-sectional nature of this dataset and other literature on this topic, a causal relationship between menthol usage and mental illness cannot yet asserted. Even still, at a pragmatic -level, these results suggest that the mental health impacts of FDA's proposed menthol ban may differ across racial/ethnic groups. In the short-term, people who use menthol cigarettes may require additional mental health support following implementation of the ban to help deal with the loss of access to a previously relied upon coping tool (i.e., menthol cigarettes). The longer-term effect of the proposed menthol ban on mental health is likely to be dependent upon the existence and nature of a causal relationship between menthol cigarette use and mental illness.

## Limitations and opportunities for future research

Because data were collected cross-sectionally, these results can not purport to represent a causal relationship between menthol cigarette use and mental illness. Future research must directly confront the potential for simultaneity bias and selection bias, potentially by identifying instrumental variables or utilizing quasi-experimental (e.g., natural experiments leveraging menthol bans) or longitudinal methods.

It is also worth noting that race/ethnicity is a proxy for the biologically consequential effects of living in a "race-conscious society" (Geronimus et al., 2006) and the diverse set of lived experiences found among persons of color (Benmarhnia et al., 2021; Castro, 2004). We do not consider race, in-and-of-itself, to be an independent, biologically important risk factor for developing mental illness and tobacco use decisions. Instead, we rely on race/ethnicity as a proxy for differences in things like discrimination, structural racism, decade's worth of targeted advertisements from the tobacco industry, sociocultural beliefs regarding tobacco and menthol use, and socioeconomic opportunity (Castro, 2004; D. R. Williams \& Sternthal, 2010). Future research should identify relevant moderators of within racial/ethnic group heterogeneity. For instance, might non-menthol smoking NH-AA individuals differ from menthol smoking NH-AA individuals in a way that engenders higher rates of mental illness? Or, is there some unobserved confounder of the association between menthol cigarette use and mental illness that is operating differently among NH-AA people who smoke compared to other groups of people who smoke? There is sparse literature on what influences the likelihood of using non-menthol cigarettes among NH-AA people who smoke or, for that matter, what predicts menthol use among groups that traditionally use non-menthol cigarettes (e.g., NH-W people who smoke). Development of a more nuanced understanding of the factors that affect menthol versus non-menthol cigarette use within and across demographic groups is warranted (Castro, 2004; Dennis et al., 2021).

We also note that the K6 and WHODAS scales, which are central components of the AMI/SMI classification scheme used in the NSDUH, are tools that have been developed for population survey research and not for diagnosing mental disorders. Further, we recognize the limitations of the psychometric properties of the K6 (Fisher et al., 2008; Kessler et al., 2002) in racially and ethnically diverse groups are well established. Utilizing an AMI/SMI classification scheme based on a review of clinical records or structured diagnostic interviews could yield different results.

It should also be noted that there was a relatively low prevalence of NH-AA people who smoke non-menthol cigarettes $(\mathrm{N}=874)$ compared to NH-AA people who smoke menthol cigarettes ( $\mathrm{N}=6,046$ ) in the NSDUH dataset. This fact highlights the need to replicate these results in samples
specifically designed to examine differences in the association of menthol cigarette use with mental illness amongst Hispanic and NH-AA people who smoke.

Finally, interpretation of these results should not purport differences between racial/ethnic groups to represent biological differences that exist independent of the social treatment of racial/ethnic minority communities and individuals. Nor should these results be used to suggest that the availability of menthol cigarettes is beneficial to the health (physical and/ or mental) of NH-AA people who smoke.

## Conclusions

Despite these limitations, the present study found that the use of menthol cigarettes is positively associated with mental illness for some people who smoke. However, results also highlight the importance of not treating people who smoke as a monolith but instead, explicitly considering the sociocultural factors that may influence tobacco use and mental health. In general, these findings suggest that menthol bans-like that proposed by the FDA-could impact mental health at a population level. However, whether that effect is positive or negative depends upon the existence and nature of the causal relationship between menthol smoking and mental illness and may be community and context-dependent.
