# Sex-Based Disparities in Health Outcomes and Pandemic-Related Stressors Among Persons with Substance Use Disorders: Findings from the National Survey on Drug Use and Health, 2021 

Brandi Moore (D) and Farzana Kapadia<br>Department of Epidemiology, New York University School of Global Public Health, New York, New York, United States


#### Abstract

Introduction: The COVID-19 pandemic disrupted healthcare access and exacerbated health disparities, particularly among persons with a substance use disorder (SUD). Research on gender and sex-based disparities in health outcomes among people with SUDs is warranted, especially following periods of economic and social shocks like the COVID-19 pandemic. The objective of this study is to examine sex-based differences in health outcomes and pandemic-related stressors among US adults with past-year SUD following the first year of the COVID-19 pandemic. Methods: Data from the 2021 National Survey on Drug Use and Health were used to examine past-year sexually transmitted infection (STI) diagnoses, emergency department visits, overnight hospital stays, serious psychological distress, and unmet mental health needs among those with SUDs. Pandemic-related stressors included frequency of serious financial worries, mental health impacts, changes to substance use, and health care access challenges. Weighted prevalence estimates for sociodemographics, SUD-type, and outcomes were calculated by sex. Multivariable logistic regression models were used to estimate adjusted prevalence odds ratios. Results: Female persons with SUDs had higher odds of adverse health outcomes-including past-year STI diagnosis ( $\mathrm{AOR}=2.00 ; 95 \% \mathrm{Cl}: 1.25-3.20$ ) and serious psychological distress ( $\mathrm{AOR}=$ 2.06; $95 \% \mathrm{Cl}: 1.72-2.47$-as well as higher odds of experiencing pandemic-related stressors including frequent, serious financial worries ( $\mathrm{AOR}=1.31 ; 95 \% \mathrm{Cl}: 1.09-1.57$ ) and increased alcohol use due to the pandemic ( $\mathrm{AOR}=1.51 ; 95 \% \mathrm{Cl}: 1.23-1.85$ ) compared to male persons. Conclusions: Following the initial waves of the COVID-19 pandemic, female persons with SUDs experienced disproportionate adverse health and pandemic-related impacts. These findings underscore the need for targeted interventions to address health and social vulnerabilities exacerbated by large-scale crises.

Disparities; substance use disorder; COVID-19; NSDUH; female; women; substance use; mental health; sexually transmitted infection

## Introduction

In 2021, following the first year of the COVID-19 pandemic, an estimated 46.3 million people in the United States had a past-year substance use disorder (SUD) (Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration (SAMHSA), 2022). Among those with a past-year SUD, $48.1 \%$ had only an alcohol use disorder, $36.1 \%$ had only a drug use disorder, and $15.8 \%$ had both an alcohol and drug use disorder (SAMHSA, 2022). Prior research has highlighted how the COVID-19 pandemic had specific, negative impacts on people with SUDs. For example, many SUD treatment programs were forced to reduce or completely cease in-person services to mitigate the spread of COVID-19, leading to delays in treatment initiation, reductions in service offerings, and reductions in program attendance (Melamed et al., 2022; Pagano et al., 2021). Further, in both hospital and primary care settings, capacity to provide care to patients with SUDs was reduced, often due to redeployment of clinical staff toward

COVID-19-related care (Herring et al., 2021; Melamed et al., 2022). Additionally, pandemic-related stressors-including financial insecurity, health care disruptions, and social service disruptions-disproportionately impacted those with SUDs, with individuals reporting worsened housing and food insecurity, physical health complications, and worsened mental health during the pandemic's first year (Russell et al., 2021; Walters et al., 2022).

While studies that explore the pandemic's impact on the overall population of persons with SUDs offer important public health insights, they may obscure variations in vulnerability that exist within the population, particularly when considering those who face additional sources of marginalization due to factors such as their gender or sex. The prevalence of SUDs has historically been higher among men, though women with SUDs are more likely to experience greater functional and medical impairments. For example, relative to men and male persons, women and female
persons with SUDs often experience more severe psychiatric symptoms and exhibit a higher prevalence of co-occurring mental health disorders including depressive, anxiety, and mood disorders (Back et al., 2011; McHugh et al., 2013, 2018; Zilberman et al., 2003). Additionally, women with SUDs report experiencing more medical problems associated with their SUD and poorer health-related quality of life compared to men (Back et al., 2011; Grella \& Lovinger, 2012; Griffin et al., 2015; Wu et al., 2010). Across studies, women are also less likely than men to initiate SUD treatment and often experience distinct barriers to treatment including childcare needs, discomfort attending mixed-gender programs due to trauma, and greater internalized stigmatization regarding their SUD (Agterberg et al., 2020; Kauffman et al., 1995).

In the context of the COVID-19 pandemic, several studies have also highlighted the disproportionate impact the crisis had on women's financial security, mental health, and substance use in the general population (Connor et al., 2020; Dang \& Nguyen, 2021; Thibaut \& EL-Nahas, 2023; Wang et al., 2020). For example, women were more likely than men to experience losses in income, work hours, and permanent employment during the pandemic's first year (Dang \& Nguyen, 2021; Lindau et al., 2021; Park et al., 2020). Additionally, several studies reported women had higher prevalences of mental health issues such as depression, anxiety, and post-traumatic stress during the initial waves of the pandemic as well as greater increases in alcohol and drug consumption relative to men (Kolakowsky-Hayner et al., 2021; Mougharbel et al., 2021; Park et al., 2020; Rodriguez et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2020).

Importantly, despite evidence indicating that women and female persons with SUDs experience heighted vulnerability to poor physical and mental health outcomes, and recent research which calls attention to gender and sex-based disparities in the health, social, and financial impacts of the COVID-19 pandemic, there remains limited empirical investigation into such disparities within the population of persons with SUDs. Because male and female persons with SUDs often experience distinct health and social challenges, it is critical to explore whether and to what extent social and economic shocks such as the COVID-19 pandemic exacerbate existing disparities and introduce new vulnerabilities within an already marginalized population. Thus, the objectives of this study are to examine sex-based differences in health outcomes and pandemic-related stressors/consequences among a nationally representative sample of persons with SUDs in the United States following the initial waves of the COVID-19 pandemic.

## Methods

## Study design and analytic sample

The analytic sample for this study was drawn from the 2021 National Survey on Drug Use and Health (NSDUH) public use file. The NSDUH is an annual, cross-sectional survey of non-institutionalized individuals aged 12 years or older living in the United States. The survey gathers nationally
representative data on individuals' substance use, mental health, general health, and health care use (SAMHSA, 2022). Due to the COVID-19 pandemic, the 2021 NSDUH was administered via in-person and online interviews with $54.6 \%$ of interviews completed online. In total, 69,850 individuals participated in the 2021 survey, with 58,304 records included in the public use file (Center for Behavioral Health Statistics \& Quality (CBHSQ), 2022). Weighted interview response rates were $38.4 \%$ for adolescents (aged 12-17) and $47.0 \%$ for adults (aged $\geq 18$ ) (SAMHSA, 2022). Further details about the NSDUH methodology are reported elsewhere (CBHSQ, 2022). Data for all respondents aged $\geq 18$ years and who had a past-year SUD were included in this analysis, representing $16.3 \%$ of the total sample from the public use file. As this study used publicly available, deidentified data, it did not require human subject approval.

## Measures

## Substance use disorders

In the 2021 NSDUH, questions used to determine past-year SUD were administered to all participants who reported past-year use of any of the following substances: alcohol, cannabis, cocaine, methamphetamine, heroin, hallucinogens, inhalants, prescription pain relievers, prescription sedatives, prescription tranquilizers, and/or prescription stimulants. Participants who indicated use of at least one substance were asked a series of questions based on the 11 criteria for SUD diagnosis specified in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, 5th edition (DSM-5), including questions designed to measure past-year dependence on the substance, social consequences of continued use, and escalating use (SAMHSA, 2022). For the majority of substances, individuals were identified as having a past-year SUD for that substance if their responses indicated they met $\geq 2$ of the 11 DSM-5 criteria. For individuals who reported use but not misuse of prescription psychotherapeutic drugs (i.e., pain relievers, sedatives, stimulants, and tranquilizers), the presence of a SUD was determined by meeting $\geq 2$ of 9DSM-5 criteria for SUD, excluding two criteria related to tolerance and withdrawal (SAMHSA, n.d.; Hasin et al., 2013). These definitions align with DSM-5 diagnostic criteria for SUD (American Psychiatric Association, 2013).

## Biological sex

For in-person interviews, the 2021 NSDUH measured respondents' sex by instructing interviewers to "record respondent's gender." For online interviews, respondents were asked "What is your sex?" For both modes of survey administration, response options were limited to either "female" or "male."

## Health outcomes

A comprehensive set of past-year health outcomes were examined including: sexually transmitted infection (STI) diagnoses, reporting $\geq 1$ emergency department visit,
reporting $\geq 1$ overnight hospital stay, reporting an unmet need for mental health treatment, and serious psychological distress. Past-year unmet need for mental health treatment was defined by participants indicating there was a time in the past 12 months when they needed mental health treatment/counseling but did not receive it. Serious psychological distress was defined based on respondents' answers to questions from the 6-item Kessler distress scale (K6)-a scale that assesses feelings of nervousness, hopelessness, worthlessness, restlessness, and depression over the past month, with a maximum score of 24 (Kessler et al., 2002). Responses were recorded on a five-point Likert scale and a combined score of $\geq 13$ was categorized as indicating serious psychological distress. In the 2021 NSDUH, K6 scores were calculated for the 30 days prior to the interview and for any month in the past 12 months where the respondent felt more depressed, anxious, or emotionally stressed than the past 30 days. The higher K-6 score was used to generate a binary variable indicating whether a respondent experienced past-year serious psychological distress.

## COVID-19 pandemic-related stressors

The following pandemic-related outcomes were assessed: having frequent serious financial worries due to the pandemic, increased use of drugs or alcohol due to the pandemic, reporting that the pandemic had a severe impact on one's emotional/mental health, and reporting moderate-severe impacts on one's health due to pandemic-related delays/cancellations in mental health or medical appointments. To assess financial worries, respondents were asked how often they had serious financial worries because of the pandemic. Responses were dichotomized to compare those who reported experiencing serious financial worries "All the time"/"Nearly all the time" to those who reported less frequent worrying. For changes in drug and alcohol use, respondents were asked to gauge how much the COVID-19 pandemic affected the amount of alcohol or other drugs they used. Those reporting they used either "a little more" or "much more" of a substance relative to pre-pandemic were compared to those reporting their substance use decreased or did not change. To assess the impacts on emotional/mental health, respondents were asked to what extent, if at all, COVID-19 negatively impacted their emotional or mental health. A binary variable was created to compare those reporting COVID-19 affected them "A lot"/" "Quite a bit" to those reporting lesser impacts. Finally, to characterize self-perceived health impacts of pandemic-related delays/cancellations in care, respondents were first asked whether they experienced any delays or cancellations in mental health or medical appointments due to the COVID-19 pandemic and, if so, whether these interruptions in care had a moderate-severe impact on their health (Yes/No).

## Statistical analyses

Weighted prevalence estimates of sociodemographic characteristics and different SUDs were calculated for the overall sample and by sex. Pearson $\chi^{2}$ tests were used to
compare characteristics between male and female respondents. Weighted prevalence estimates of all health and pandemic-related outcomes were also calculated by sex. Logistic regression was used to calculate unadjusted and adjusted prevalence odds ratios to estimate the association between respondents' sex and the outcomes-of-interest. All multivariable models included the following covariates: age (categorical), race/ethnicity, marital status, highest level of education, health insurance status (insured/uninsured), employment status, and poverty level (income below federal poverty level, income between 100 and $200 \%$ of poverty level, or income $>200 \%$ of poverty level). Unadjusted and adjusted odds ratios (AOR) and accompanying 95\% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported for all models. Analyses were conducted with Stata/SE 18 and all accounted for the 2021 NSDUH's complex survey design (CBHSQ, 2022).

## Results

## Overview

The unweighted analytic sample consisted of 9,465 adults classified as having a past-year SUD, equivalent to $44,412,670$ individuals when applying sample weights and $17.50 \%$ of the total adult population ( $95 \%$ CI: $16.77 \%-18.25 \%$ ). Of these 9,465 individuals, $50.45 \%$ were male respondents (unweighted $n: 4,775$; weighted $n: 25,042,579$ ) and $49.55 \%$ were female respondents (unweighted $n: 4,690$; weighted $n$ : $19,370,091)$. Table 1 presents weighted prevalence estimates for sociodemographic characteristics of the total adult population of people with SUDs as well as sex-specific prevalence estimates. Briefly, an estimated $19.64 \%$ of the population was aged 18-25 years, $23.11 \%$ aged 26-34, $26.71 \%$ aged $35-49,20.59 \%$ aged $50-64$, and $9.95 \%$ aged $\geq 65$. For race/ethnicity, $63.85 \%$ of the population were Non-Hispanic (NH) White, $12.63 \% \mathrm{NH}$ Black, $2.88 \% \mathrm{NH}$ Asian, $1.01 \%$ NH Native American or Alaskan Native, $0.45 \%$ NH Pacific Islander or Native Hawaiian, $3.15 \%$ NH multiracial, and $16.04 \%$ Hispanic. In terms of SUD severity, $56.68 \%$ of the population had a mild SUD (i.e., met 2-3 DSM-5 criteria), $22.80 \%$ had a moderate SUD ( $4-5$ criteria), and $20.52 \%$ had a severe SUD ( $\geq 6$ criteria). There were significant differences ( $\mathrm{p}<0.05$ ) between male and female persons in regard to race/ethnicity, marital status, highest education level, health insurance status, poverty level status, and employment status (Table 1).

## Health outcomes

In both unadjusted and adjusted models, female persons with SUDs had higher odds than male persons of reporting a past-year STI diagnosis ( $\mathrm{AOR}=2.00 ; 95 \%$ CI: $1.25-3.20$ ), $\geq 1$ past-year emergency department visit (AOR $=1.27 ; 95 \%$ CI: 1.01-1.59), and $\geq 1$ past-year overnight hospital stay ( $\mathrm{AOR}=1.23 ; 95 \% \mathrm{CI}: 0.93-1.62$ ). For outcomes related to mental health, female persons with SUDs had approximately twice the odds of experiencing both past-year serious psychological distress ( $\mathrm{AOR}=2.06 ; 95 \%$ CI: $1.72-2.47$ ) and an unmet need for mental health treatment in the past year (AOR $=2.06 ; 95 \%$ CI: $1.66-2.55$ ) compared to male persons (Table 2).

## COVID-19 pandemic-related stressors

Female persons with SUDs were more likely to report pandemic-related stressors compared to male respondents for each outcome examined (Table 2). Relative to male respondents, female respondents had higher odds of experiencing serious financial worries all/nearly all the time due to the pandemic $(\mathrm{AOR}=1.31 ; 95 \% \mathrm{CI}: 1.09-1.57)$ and higher odds of reporting increased alcohol intake (AOR $=1.51$; $95 \% \mathrm{CI}: 1.23-1.85$ ) and drug use ( $\mathrm{AOR}=1.20 ; 95 \% \mathrm{CI}$ : $0.98-1.47)$ due to the pandemic. Additionally, female persons with SUDs also had higher odds of reporting that the pandemic impacted their mental health "A lot"/Quite a bit" (AOR $=1.67 ; 95 \%$ CI: 1.34-2.08), and higher odds of reporting that delays/cancellations of mental health appointments (AOR $=1.36 ; 95 \%$ CI: 1.01-1.83) and medical appointments
( $\mathrm{AOR}=1.79 ; 95 \%$ CI: 1.25-2.57) during the COVID-19 pandemic resulted in moderate-severe health impacts.

## Discussion

The objective of this study was to examine sex-based differences in health outcomes and pandemic-related stressors/ consequences among a nationally representative sample of adults with past-year SUDs following the initial waves of the COVID-19 pandemic. For both male and female persons with SUDs, there was high prevalence ( $>15 \%$ ) of past-year emergency department visits, serious psychological distress, frequent pandemic-related financial worries, increases in alcohol intake due to the pandemic, and pandemic-related impacts on mental health. Still, for almost all health outcomes examined, female persons with SUDs were more likely to report experiencing adverse physical and mental health outcomes, as well as more severe pandemic-related impacts on their finances, substance use, mental health, and general health. These findings are consistent with previous research which indicates that, although men/male persons tend to have a higher overall prevalence of SUDs, women/ female persons with SUDs often experience more severe adverse health consequences (Back et al., 2011; McHugh et al., 2013, 2018; Zilberman et al., 2003).

The most pronounced disparities we observed included past-year STI diagnosis and several mental health outcomes where female persons with SUDs had twice the odds of these adverse outcomes compared to their male counterparts. While studies on general populations consistently report higher prevalence and incidence of STIs for female compared to male persons, female persons with SUDs may face distinct vulnerabilities, particularly during times of upheaval such as the COVID-19 pandemic (Van Gerwen et al., 2022). [^0]SUDs have reported numerous barriers to supporting their sexual health including an inability to routinely access sexual health services, challenges balancing competing needs like housing insecurity or psychiatric comorbidities, experiences with intimate partner violence, and concerns about stigmatization due to their substance use or sexual histories (Edelman et al., 2014; MacAfee et al., 2020; Strathdee et al., 2021). Additionally, female persons with SUDs may be more likely than male persons to engage in transactional sex for money/resources, especially given the pandemic's disproportionate impact on women's employment and financial security (Dang \& Nguyen, 2021; Ellis et al., 2023). While type of STI, sexual behaviors, and sexual histories are not collected in the 2021 NSDUH, research investigating how sexual health vulnerabilities are specifically produced for female persons with SUDs during crises such as pandemics is needed to better understand opportunities for intervention and support.

Our findings also align with those of studies conducted pre-pandemic which consistently report that women and/or female persons with SUDs had higher prevalence of co-occurring psychiatric disorders and more severe mental health symptoms compared to their counterparts who are male and/or men (Back et al., 2011; McHugh et al., 2013, 2018; Zilberman et al., 2003). Additionally, these findings corroborate those which report that women generally experienced higher levels of posttraumatic stress, depression, anxiety, pandemic-related stress, and increases in alcohol intake relative to men during the initial waves of the COVID-19 pandemic (Kolakowsky-Hayner et al., 2021; Mougharbel et al., 2021; Park et al., 2020; Rodriguez et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2020). Possible mechanisms for how the pandemic may have exacerbated preexisting sex and gender disparities in mental health outcomes are numerous and multifaceted. For example, traditionally women-dominated fields (e.g., healthcare, education) were disproportionately impacted by the pandemic and women faced greater financial insecurity and income loss during lockdowns-factors that could worsen mental health, particularly among individuals who are already economically vulnerable (Collins et al., 2021; Connor et al., 2020; Dang \& Nguyen, 2021). Additionally, the pandemic and accompanying lockdowns increased many women's exposure to intimate partner violence while also reducing opportunities for support-seeking (Piquero et al., 2021). Prior research has highlighted the high prevalence of current and lifetime intimate partner violence for women with SUDs and this was likely worsened during the pandemic (Piquero et al., 2021; Schneider et al., 2009; Velez et al., 2006). Finally, women have been found to take on greater caregiving responsibilities and spend more hours caring for children, elderly relatives, and ill-loved ones relative to men (Pinquart \& Sorensen, 2006; Wade et al., 2021; Zwar et al., 2023). Caregiver burden has been consistently associated with reporting higher stress and a higher prevalence of mental health symptoms (Park et al., 2020; Pinquart \& Sorensen, 2006; Wade et al., 2021; Zwar et al., 2023). This may be exacerbated for women with SUDs who often report experiencing stress, anxiety, and fear while attempting to balance caretaking
responsibilities/desires with mental health and substance use-related needs and treatment (Adams et al., 2021; Greenfield et al., 2013; Kauffman et al., 1995).

Ultimately, our findings call for public health interventions which not only support the health and well-being of all persons with SUDs during times of prolonged crisis, but that are also tailored to address the disproportionate burdens faced by women and female persons with SUDs. For example, expansions in remote and telehealth treatment of SUDs, mental health, and physical health conditions helped bridge a critical gap in health services that occurred during lockdowns. In prior studies, women who used substances identified low-threshold telehealth/remote services as an important facilitator of service engagement due to its enhanced convenience, safety, and ability to foster social connections during the pandemic (Hurley et al., 2021; Patel et al., 2024; Russell et al., 2021). Because our findings indicate that female persons with SUDs were more likely to report moderate-severe health impacts due to an inability to access mental health or medical care, current telehealth infrastructure must be further developed to improve access for more vulnerable individuals. While health policy changes that expanded coverage for telehealth appointments were essential for improving the accessibility of remote care, other strategies such as the distribution of free/low-cost smart devices or phones, expansions in free internet access, community-based private spaces for telehealth/remote patients, audio-only telehealth options, and targeted outreach for potential patients may also improve uptake of this service option for female persons with SUDs during times of widespread health care disruptions (Talal et al., 2020).

The implementation of low-threshold services specifically tailored to women with SUDs could also reduce the likelihood of adverse mental health and substance use outcomes during future crises. Prior studies have highlighted the success of gender-responsive SUD treatment programs with participants reporting greater satisfaction with their treatment, greater improvements in psychiatric symptoms, and sustained reductions in SUD severity when compared to participants of mixed-gender groups (Evans et al., 2013; Greenfield et al., 2007; Johnstone et al., 2023). In qualitative studies, women who participate in gender-responsive programs report feeling that such programs are more effective at reducing feelings of stigmatization, responding to unmet needs, addressing traumas, and empowering participants (Greenfield et al., 2013; Johnstone et al., 2023; Kauffman et al., 1995). During times of severe disruption, these pro-grams-whether held remotely or in-person-could serve as important venues for providing resources/support for factors such as intimate partner violence, financial insecurity, and caretaking challenges that exacerbate gender and sex-based disparities in health outcomes during crises (Agterberg et al., 2020; Connor et al., 2020). Ultimately, gender-responsive programs could play a critical role in preventing adverse health, mental health, and crisis-related stressors for women and female persons with SUDs, so increasing access and funding for such programs is an important step toward addressing the disparities identified in the present study (Perri et al., 2022).

## Limitations

These results must be interpreted in the context of several key limitations. First, most data were self-reported and underreporting of behaviors/outcomes-particularly those that are stigmatized like substance use or STI diagnoses-is possible. Second, as the NSDUH only covers residents of households and those living in non-institutionalized settings, individuals who may have even greater vulnerability to adverse health outcomes and pandemic-related stressorssuch as those who are incarcerated, unstably housed, or residing in mental health institutions-are not captured in this analysis. Third, the cross-sectional nature of the NSDUH precluded a more careful assessment of the timing of outcomes relative to the start of the pandemic and the onset of SUDs.

There are also critical limitations related to the measurement of sex and gender. In 2021, the NSDUH categorized survey respondents as either male or female. While this categorization is frequently referred to as respondents' gender in official survey documentation, respondents' actual gender identities were not captured. Respondents of the web-delivered survey were explicitly asked to report their sex. In contrast, interviewers administering in-person surveys were instructed to record respondents' gender as either male or female and it is unclear whether this was determined by the interviewer themselves or directly asked to respondents. Additionally, there were no questions assessing whether respondents were cisgender or transgender. The lack of data on respondents' actual gender identities precluded more nuanced examinations of potential disparities.

Finally, due to methodological changes specific to the 2021 NSDUH, data could not be combined with other survey years to create a more robust analytic sample or to observe differences in the prevalence of outcomes before and after the COVID-19 pandemic. Given that the 2021 survey was the first to include questions related to COVID-19 and that the purpose of this analysis was to examine immediate impacts of the pandemic, the inability to combine multiple years of survey data was of less concern.

## Conclusions

In the year after the initial waves of COVID-19, female persons with SUDs were more likely to report adverse health outcomes and pandemic-related stressors compared to male persons. These findings highlight the importance of examining the consequences of massive social/economic shocks, not only for vulnerable populations generally but to also compare outcomes within such populations. As such, future research should investigate whether and to what extent similar outcomes vary by race, ethnicity, socioeconomic status, and/or type of SUD. By advancing our understanding of the intricacies of health and social vulnerabilities among persons with SUDs, more tailored interventions can be implemented which better address the unique needs that different groups may have in the aftermath of intensely disruptive events.
