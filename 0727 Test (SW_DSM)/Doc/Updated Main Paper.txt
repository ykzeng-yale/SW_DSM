\abstract[Abstract]{Double score matching is an emerging method for causal inference that combines propensity and prognostic scores to achieve dimension reduction and model robustness. However, when the observational data are drawn from a complex survey, the existing literature does not provide theoretical guidance on the proper use of survey weights in matching for population-level causal inference. We develop weighted double score matching (WDSM) that provides a unified framework for incorporating survey weights throughout the score estimation, matching, and outcome analysis under both retrospective (informative) and prospective (non-informative) sampling designs. We establish asymptotic normality under both known and estimated scores and develop a linearization-based wild bootstrap procedure for variance estimation that preserves the survey-weighted matching structure. Through an extensive series of simulation studies, we examined the performance of our estimators and compared the results to those of alternative methods. We carried out two case studies to illustrate the application of the proposed methods with complex survey data. We concluded with a discussion of our findings and provided practical guidelines for weighted double score matching analysis of observational data from complex surveys.}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Observational data are frequently used to estimate the causal effects of treatments, exposures, and interventions across medical and social sciences, particularly when randomized trials are unavailable, unethical, or resource-prohibitive. One key limitation of observational data is that the lack of randomization can introduce confounding: systematic differences in baseline characteristics between exposed and unexposed groups may bias the estimated treatment effect.\cite{Rosenbaum2002} Balancing score methods, especially the propensity score, defined as the probability of receiving treatment given observed covariates,\cite{Li2018, rosenbaum1983central} have emerged as a popular approach for addressing baseline confounding in observational studies. Propensity score can be incorporated to improve baseline balance through three main approaches, including matching,\cite{Stuart2010}, weighting,\cite{Li2018}, and stratification or subclassification,\cite{Lunceford2004}, all of which help form comparable exposed and unexposed groups to reduce bias in the estimated treatment effect.

Collecting real-world observational data often employ complex survey designs such as  multistage sampling, geographic stratification, cluster sampling, and deliberate oversampling of less representative subgroups to ensure population representativeness\cite{LaVange2001Applying,lumley2011complex} Large-scale national surveys such as the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) and the National Survey on Drug Use and Health (NSDUH) exemplify such designs and are widely used in health research to estimate causal effects of exposures and health behaviors. Survey weights already computed in these data collections are integral to recovering population features so that the differentiated selection probabilities and non-response patterns are appropriately accounted for to attain findings that are generalizable to the population of the United States. The proper incorporation of survey weights is especially critical in retrospective sampling designs, where survey weights may depend on both exposure status and covariates.\cite{Ridgeway2015,salerno2025whatsweight} The need to combine complex survey designs with propensity score methods for valid population-level causal inference has motivated substantial methodological development.\cite{zeng2025movingbestpracticeusing, Zanutto2006,DuGoff2014,Ridgeway2015, Yang2023} For instance, Salerno et al.\cite{salerno2025whatsweight} explored the performance of propensity score weighting methods under complex survey mechanism in health disparities research where survey weights depend on the exposure variable of interest, such as race, and showed through simulations that whether the propensity score should be survey-weighted depends on whether the available survey weights are tied to comparison variables such as treatment or exposure. More recently, Zeng et al.\cite{zeng2025movingbestpracticeusing} developed a unified framework that derives the methodological foundations for both retrospective and prospective sampling designs under the balancing weights framework.

%For stratification and subclassification, Zanutto et al.\cite{ZanuttoLuHornik2005} and Zanutto\cite{Zanutto2006} recommended incorporating survey weights when estimating treatment effects within strata but not in propensity score estimation itself. For propensity score matching, however, the appropriate use of survey weights remains contentious and theoretically underdeveloped. DuGoff et al.\cite{DuGoff2014} suggested that survey weights can carry unique design features that are not captured by observed covariates, and therefore should be included as an additional covariate in the propensity score model to enhance covariate balance and address potential bias due to unmeasured covariates, extending their recommendations to both matching and weighting implementations. Austin et al.\cite{austin2018propensity} illustrated through simulations that including survey weight as a covariate in the propensity score model showed no clear advantage over a survey-weighted propensity score model, and that neither approach can consistently outperform the other regarding covariate balance and bias in the treatment effect estimation across various scenarios. Dong et al.\cite{DongStuartLenisNguyen2020} evaluated multiple strategies for incorporating survey weights into both propensity score and outcome models, recommending their use at both stages, although they observed that estimates from different strategies did not yield apparent differences in their data application. 
However, methodological recommendations for propensity score matching in complex survey studies have been less consistent. Through simulation studies, Lenis et al.\cite{Lenis2019} suggested that incorporating survey weights in the propensity score model does not necessarily improve estimation of the population-level treatment effect using matching, especially when sufficient population-level balance in confounders can already be achieved. Unlike weighting and stratification, matching introduces the additional challenge of weight assignment for matched control subjects, a problem unique to the matching framework:\cite{austin2018propensity} Austin et al.\cite{austin2018propensity} demonstrated that ``natural weights,'' where matched controls retain their original survey weights, generally achieve superior covariate balance and reduced bias, while Lenis et al.\cite{Lenis2019} provided theoretical justification for ``inherited weights'' in population average treatment effect on the treated (PATT) estimation, arguing that transferring treated individuals' weights to matched controls ensures the weighted covariate distribution among comparison individuals replicates the treated population distribution. All existing work on survey weights in matching has focused exclusively on propensity score matching under prospective sampling designs, leaving two critical gaps: no consensus on optimal survey weight incorporation at each stage of the matching procedure, and no framework extending beyond propensity score matching to broader matching methods or informative retrospective sampling where selection depends on both covariates and treatment status.

More recently, double score matching (DSM) has emerged as a powerful extension of single-score matching by combining propensity and prognostic scores to achieve both improved efficiency and double robustness against model misspecification. Leacy and Stuart\cite{LeacyStuart2014} first demonstrated the empirical benefits of matching jointly on propensity and prognostic scores, showing that the use of both scores improves covariate balance and reduces bias compared to propensity score matching alone. Antonelli et al.\cite{Antonelli2018} provided rigorous theoretical foundations establishing consistency and convergence rates under single-model correctness, proving that double score matching is consistent when either the propensity score model or the prognostic score model is correctly specified. Yang and Zhang\cite{yang_multiply_2023} formalized double score matching within a complete asymptotic framework that includes multiply robust matching and bias-corrected estimation, and demonstrated that matching on treatment-specific double scores reduces the matching dimension to two, achieving matching bias of order $O(n^{-1/2})$ that is comparable to parametric rates. These developments establish DSM as a state-of-the-art matching framework offering both theoretical robustness and superior finite-sample performance. However, all existing developments in double score matching assume simple random sampling, without addressing how survey weights should be incorporated into score construction, the matching procedure, or the outcome analysis within matched sets, leaving practitioners without principled guidance for population-level double-robust matching that properly accounts for complex survey designs.

This paper aims to contribute to the literature on matching methods for survey observational data in several ways. First, we establish a unified framework that incorporates survey weights into double score construction, matching, and outcome analysis for both retrospective (informative) and prospective (non-informative) sampling designs, targeting PATE with extensions to PATT. Second, we prove consistency and asymptotic normality of the proposed weighted double score matching (WDSM) estimators, demonstrating that WDSM preserves double robustness when either the propensity or prognostic model is correctly specified under survey-weighted estimation. Third, we develop a computationally efficient bootstrap variance estimation procedure that addresses the dual challenges of non-smooth matching functionals and complex survey designs. Lastly, given that no consensus has been reached on the best use of survey weights in matching analysis under various real-world scenarios, we conduct extensive simulations examining varying overlap conditions, model misspecification patterns, and sampling designs, and carry out two case studies to illustrate the application of the proposed methods with complex survey data.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec: notation and assumption} defines the notation and assumptions for the potential outcomes framework in the context of survey observational data, introducing survey ignorability conditions and distinguishing retrospective from prospective sampling designs. Section~\ref{sec:method} develops the WDSM estimator for PATE through survey-weighted imputation within match sets, derives the bias decomposition and asymptotic theory under both known and estimated scores, and presents the bootstrap variance estimation procedure. Section~\ref{sec:PATT extension} extends the framework to PATT, which requires only one-sided ignorability and control-side modeling. Section~\ref{sec:simulation} conducts simulations to examine the performance of different matching estimators incorporating survey weights across varying overlap conditions and sampling designs. Section~\ref{sec:case studies} compares the performance of the different matching procedures in two real data examples, and Section~\ref{sec:discussion} concludes.








\section{Notation and Assumptions}\label{sec: notation and assumption}


\subsection{Causal Inference Setup and Basic Notation}


Consider a survey sample of size $n$ drawn from a finite population $\Omega$ of size $N$, with each sampled individual indexed by $i = 1, \ldots, n$. We observe a $d$-dimensional vector of pre-treatment covariates $\mathbf{X}_i \in \mathcal{X} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^d$, and a binary treatment indicator $Z_i \in \{0,1\}$ that indicates treatment ($Z_i = 1$) or control ($Z_i = 0$) group affiliation. Under the Stable Unit Treatment Value Assumption (SUTVA), the potential outcomes $Y_i(z)$ for $z \in \{0,1\}$ are assumed to be well-defined, and the observed outcome for each individual is $Y_i = Z_i Y_i(1) + (1-Z_i) Y_i(0)$.\cite{Rubin1974}
For estimating population effects using our sample, we define the survey selection indicator $S_i$ where $S_i = 1$ denotes selection into the sample and $S_i = 0$ otherwise. We assume that $(Y_i(0), Y_i(1), \mathbf{X}_i, Z_i, S_i)$ are defined on a superpopulation law $P_\Omega$, and the observed sample $\{(\mathbf{X}_i, Z_i, Y_i)\}_{i=1}^n$ consists of independent and identically distributed draws from the selected-sample law $P_s(\cdot) = P_\Omega(\cdot \mid S_i = 1)$, with treatment-specific sample sizes $n_1 = \sum_{i=1}^n Z_i$ and $n_0 = \sum_{i=1}^n (1-Z_i)$. 
For notational convenience, we denote the propensity score $e(\mathbf{X}_i) = P(Z_i = 1 \mid \mathbf{X}_i)$, the outcome mean functions $\mu_z(\mathbf{X}_i) = E[Y_i(z) \mid \mathbf{X}_i]$, and the conditional variance functions $\sigma_z^2(\mathbf{X}_i) = \operatorname{Var}(Y_i(z) \mid \mathbf{X}_i)$ for $z \in \{0,1\}$, where all expectations are taken under $P_\Omega$.

Our primary estimands are population-level causal parameters, the PATE $\tau_{\text{PATE}} = E[Y_i(1) - Y_i(0)]$ and the PATT $\tau_{\text{PATT}} = E[Y_i(1) - Y_i(0) \mid Z_i = 1]$. These differ from their sample analogs, and recovering them from the survey sample requires both causal identification conditions and proper incorporation of survey weights.\cite{dugoff2014generalizing,ridgeway2015propensity} For causal identification, we assume \textit{treatment unconfoundedness}: $(Y_i(0), Y_i(1)) \perp Z_i \mid \mathbf{X}_i$, and \textit{treatment positivity}: $0 < e(\mathbf{X}_i) < 1$ almost surely. For PATT estimation, unconfoundedness can be relaxed to the one-sided condition $Y_i(0) \perp Z_i \mid \mathbf{X}_i$, and positivity to the requirement that $e(\mathbf{X}_i) > 0$ implies $1 - e(\mathbf{X}_i) > 0$.\cite{Heckman1997,yang_multiply_2023} The survey weight conditions are developed in Section~\ref{subsec:survey_design}. We present the methodology for PATE under retrospective (informative) sampling as the general case and provide extensions to PATT in Section~\ref{sec:PATT extension} and prospective (non-informative) sampling results in Web Appendix B.1.




\subsection{Survey Design Frameworks and Weight Structures} \label{subsec:survey_design}


To estimate population-level causal parameters from the survey sample, the sampling mechanism that generated the observed data must be properly characterized. Following Zeng et al.\cite{zeng2025movingbestpracticeusing} and Salerno et al.,\cite{salerno2025whatsweight} we distinguish two sampling regimes based on when sampling occurs relative to treatment assignment, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:sampling_diagram}. We begin with the retrospective framework as the general case and present the prospective counterpart as a special case.


% \subsubsection{Retrospective Sampling Design}


Retrospective designs arise when treatment assignment precedes sampling and the sampling mechanism depends on both treatment status and covariates. Examples include surveys where the survey weights are tied to the comparison variable,\cite{salerno2025whatsweight} such as NHANES oversampling by race when race is the exposure of interest, and surveys whose design weights carry features associated with the treatment variable.\cite{DuGoff2014} To identify population-level treatment effects under this design, we require the following conditions on the sampling mechanism.

\begin{assumption}[Retrospective Survey Ignorability]
\label{ass:retro_ignorability}
Under retrospective sampling: (a) sampling probabilities depend on both covariates and treatment status, $\pi_z(\mathbf{x}_i) = P(S_i = 1 \mid \mathbf{X}_i = \mathbf{x}_i, Z_i = z)$ for $z \in \{0,1\}$, and we denote the treatment-specific survey weights as $w_z(\mathbf{x}_i) = 1/\pi_z(\mathbf{x}_i)$, which are known from the survey design; (b) survey positivity holds, $0 < \pi_z(\mathbf{X}_i) < 1$ almost surely for $z \in \{0,1\}$; and (c) selection is conditionally independent of potential outcomes given covariates and treatment, $(Y_i(0), Y_i(1)) \perp S_i \mid \mathbf{X}_i, Z_i$.
\end{assumption}

% Assumption~\ref{ass:retro_ignorability}(a) specifies that the sampling probabilities may differ across treatment groups for individuals with the same covariates, necessitating treatment-specific weights to recover population quantities. Assumption~\ref{ass:retro_ignorability}(b) guarantees that all covariate-treatment combinations have positive probability of being sampled. Assumption~\ref{ass:retro_ignorability}(c) ensures that the survey selection does not introduce systematic bias in the potential outcomes beyond what is captured by $\mathbf{X}_i$ and $Z_i$.
Because the sampling probabilities may differ across treatment groups for individuals with the same covariates, treatment-specific weights are necessary to recover population quantities from the survey sample.
% \subsubsection{Prospective Sampling Design}
In contrast, prospective designs arise when survey selection takes place before treatment assignment. In this case, the sampling mechanism depends only on pre-treatment covariates and satisfies the conditional independence $S_i \perp Z_i \mid \mathbf{X}_i$, so that the treatment-specific sampling probabilities reduce to a common probability $\pi_1(\mathbf{x}_i) = \pi_0(\mathbf{x}_i) = \pi(\mathbf{x}_i)$. In certain prospective settings, this conditional independence may not hold, for example when a treatment is only accessible within the sampled population but unavailable to the broader target population. Such settings fall under the scope of generalizability and transportability\cite{dahabreh2020extending,stuart2011use} and are not the focus of this paper, though our framework can be extended to accommodate them. To identify population-level treatment effects under this design, we require the following conditions.

\begin{assumption}[Prospective Survey Ignorability]
\label{ass:prosp_ignorability}
Under prospective sampling: (a) sampling probabilities depend only on pre-treatment covariates, $\pi(\mathbf{x}_i) = P(S_i = 1 \mid \mathbf{X}_i = \mathbf{x}_i)$, and we denote the common survey weights as $w(\mathbf{x}_i) = 1/\pi(\mathbf{x}_i)$, which are known from the survey design; (b) survey positivity holds, $0 < \pi(\mathbf{X}_i) < 1$ almost surely; and (c) selection is conditionally independent of treatment and potential outcomes given covariates, $(Z_i, Y_i(0), Y_i(1)) \perp S_i \mid \mathbf{X}_i$.
\end{assumption}

Assumption~\ref{ass:prosp_ignorability}(c) is stronger than the retrospective counterpart and implies that the sample-level propensity score coincides with the population-level propensity score, $P(Z_i = 1 \mid \mathbf{X}_i, S_i = 1) = P(Z_i = 1 \mid \mathbf{X}_i) = e(\mathbf{X}_i)$.

\subsection{Double Score Construction and Balancing Properties}



Following Yang and Zhang,\cite{yang_multiply_2023} we construct treatment-specific matching variables using population-level scores that achieve dimension reduction while maintaining balancing score properties for population treatment effect estimation. We define the prognostic score $\Psi_z(\mathbf{X}_i) = E[Y_i(z) \mid \mathbf{X}_i]$ for $z \in \{0,1\}$ as a sufficient statistic for $Y_i(z)$ in the sense that $Y_i(z) \perp \mathbf{X}_i \mid \Psi_z(\mathbf{X}_i)$, which coincides with the outcome mean function $\mu_z(\mathbf{X}_i)$ under the prognostic sufficiency condition.\cite{Hansen2008,yang_multiply_2023} Combining the population-level propensity score and arm-specific prognostic scores, we define the treatment-specific double score for PATE as
\[
\mathbf{D}_z(\mathbf{X}_i) = (e(\mathbf{X}_i),\, \Psi_z(\mathbf{X}_i)) \in \mathbb{R}^2, \qquad z \in \{0,1\},
\]
and for PATT only the one-sided control score $\mathbf{D}_0(\mathbf{X}_i) = (e(\mathbf{X}_i),\, \Psi_0(\mathbf{X}_i))$ is required. Rather than matching on high-dimensional full covariates, the treatment-specific double score reduces the matching dimension to two, yielding matching bias of order $O(n^{-1/2})$ after bias correction that is comparable to parametric rates.\cite{yang_multiply_2023}


Matching on the double score is justified by the following balancing property, established by Antonelli et al.\cite{Antonelli2018} and refined by Yang and Zhang\cite{yang_multiply_2023} for treatment-specific scores, which guarantees that confounding due to $\mathbf{X}_i$ is removed within strata defined by the two-dimensional score $\mathbf{D}_z(\mathbf{X}_i)$.

\begin{lemma}[Treatment-Specific Double Score Balancing Properties; Antonelli et al.\cite{Antonelli2018}, Yang and Zhang\cite{yang_multiply_2023}]\label{lem:ds_balancing}
Under treatment unconfoundedness $(Y_i(0), Y_i(1)) \perp Z_i \mid \mathbf{X}_i$: (a) $Y_i(z) \perp Z_i \mid \{h(\mathbf{X}_i),\, \Psi_z(\mathbf{X}_i)\}$, and (b) $Y_i(z) \perp Z_i \mid \{e(\mathbf{X}_i),\, h(\mathbf{X}_i)\}$ for any $h(\mathbf{X}_i)$ and $z \in \{0,1\}$. Together (a) and (b) yield (c) $Y_i(z) \perp Z_i \mid \mathbf{D}_z(\mathbf{X}_i)$ for $z \in \{0,1\}$. For PATT, under the one-sided condition $Y_i(0) \perp Z_i \mid \mathbf{X}_i$, similarly we have $Y_i(0) \perp Z_i \mid \mathbf{D}_0(\mathbf{X}_i)$.
\end{lemma}

Lemma~\ref{lem:ds_balancing}(c) implies that matching on the double score achieves population-level covariate balance between treatment groups, with $E[Y_i(z)] = E[E(Y_i \mid Z_i = z, \mathbf{D}_z(\mathbf{X}_i))]$ for $z \in \{0,1\}$, so that each individual's counterfactual outcome can be imputed from matched individuals with the same score value in the opposite arm. In survey observational data, the population-level scores must be properly estimated from the survey sample, and the matched-set imputation must incorporate survey weights for valid estimation of population treatment effects. The score estimation requirements and the construction of the survey-weighted matching estimator are developed in Section~\ref{sec:method}. Additional regularity conditions on the double score are detailed in Web Appendix A.

\section{Method}\label{sec:method} 

This section develops the WDSM methodology for retrospective PATE as the general case, covering double score estimation, estimator construction, bias decomposition, asymptotic theory, and variance estimation. Extensions to PATT are provided in Section~\ref{sec:PATT extension}, and prospective sampling counterparts are collected in the Web Appendix B.


\subsection{Double Score Model Specification}\label{subsec:score_models}

In practice, the population-level double scores $\mathbf{D}_z(\mathbf{X}_i) = (e(\mathbf{X}_i), \Psi_z(\mathbf{X}_i))$ defined in Section~\ref{sec: notation and assumption} are unknown and must be estimated from the observed data to construct the matching variables. Following the empirical literature,\cite{LeacyStuart2014,yang_multiply_2023} we posit a logistic regression model for the propensity score, $e(\mathbf{X}; \boldsymbol{\alpha}) = \operatorname{expit}(\mathbf{X}^T \boldsymbol{\alpha})$, and linear regression models for the prognostic scores, $\Psi_z(\mathbf{X}; \boldsymbol{\beta}_z) = \mathbf{X}^T \boldsymbol{\beta}_z$ for $z \in \{0,1\}$, though flexible semiparametric specifications can also be accommodated. We collect all score parameters into $\boldsymbol{\theta} = (\boldsymbol{\alpha}^T, \boldsymbol{\beta}_0^T, \boldsymbol{\beta}_1^T)^T$. A key question in the survey setting is whether the estimation of each score component requires survey weights, which depends on the sampling design as follows.

Under retrospective sampling, the sample-level propensity score $P(Z_i = 1 \mid \mathbf{X}_i, S_i = 1)$ differs from the population propensity score $e(\mathbf{X}_i)$ due to treatment-dependent sampling (Assumption~\ref{ass:retro_ignorability}), and survey-weighted estimation is required to recover the population propensity score. The parameter $\widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}$ solves the survey-weighted estimating equation
\begin{align}\label{eq:ps_ee}
\sum_{i=1}^n w_{Z_i}(\mathbf{X}_i)\, \mathbf{X}_i \{Z_i - e(\mathbf{X}_i; \boldsymbol{\alpha})\} = 0.
\end{align}
In contrast, for prognostic score estimation, Assumption~\ref{ass:retro_ignorability}(c) and treatment unconfoundedness together imply $E[Y_i \mid \mathbf{X}_i, Z_i = z, S_i = 1] = E[Y_i \mid \mathbf{X}_i, Z_i = z] = \Psi_z(\mathbf{X}_i)$, so the population prognostic score can be consistently estimated directly from the observed sample data within each treatment arm without survey weights. The parameter $\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_z$ solves
\begin{align}\label{eq:pgs_ee}
\sum_{i: Z_i=z} \mathbf{X}_i \{Y_i - \Psi_z(\mathbf{X}_i; \boldsymbol{\beta}_z)\} = 0.
\end{align}
Under prospective sampling, with Assumption~\ref{ass:prosp_ignorability}(c), the sample-level and population-level propensity scores coincide, and both score components can be estimated without survey weights.

\subsection{WDSM Estimator Construction}\label{subsec:simple estimator}

Given the estimated double scores $\widehat{\mathbf{D}}_z(\mathbf{X}_i) = (\widehat{e}(\mathbf{X}_i), \widehat{\Psi}_z(\mathbf{X}_i))$ obtained in Section~\ref{subsec:score_models}, we construct the WDSM estimator through matching, survey-weighted imputation, and Hájek-normalized aggregation. Following Yang and Zhang,\cite{yang_multiply_2023} we estimate $\tau_{\mathrm{PATE}} = E[Y(1)] - E[Y(0)]$ by estimating $E[Y(z)]$ separately for $z = 0, 1$, aggregating potential outcomes across individuals in both treatment groups. 
For each individual $i$, if $Z_i = z$, the potential outcome $Y_i(z) = Y_i$ is directly observed; if $Z_i = 1-z$, the potential outcome $Y_i(z)$ is not observed and needs to be imputed from individuals in arm $z$ with similar covariates, which is achieved by matching on the arm-specific double score $\widehat{\mathbf{D}}_z(\mathbf{X})$, since Lemma~\ref{lem:ds_balancing}(c) and SUTVA together imply $E[Y(z) \mid Z = 1-z, \mathbf{D}_z(\mathbf{X}_i)] = E[Y \mid Z = z, \mathbf{D}_z(\mathbf{X}_i)]$.
% To further explain why matching on \mathbf{D}_z(\mathbf{X})) in arm z can approximate the target E[E(Y(z) \mid Z = 1-z,\, \mathbf{D}_z(\mathbf{X}))] for individual in arm 1-z
% \begin{align}\label{eq:identification}

To perform this imputation, for each individual $i$ with $Z_i \neq z$, we find the $M$ nearest neighbors from arm $z$ based on the Euclidean distance on the standardized double score space $\widehat{\mathbf{D}}_z(\mathbf{X})$, where each component is normalized to have mean zero and unit variance for numerical stability,\cite{abadie2006} and denote the index set of these $M$ matched individuals by $\mathcal{J}_{\mathbf{D}_z}(i)$.
% Formally, the index set of these $M$ matched individuals defined as
% \begin{align}\label{eq:matchset}
% \mathcal{J}_{\mathbf{D}_z}(i) = \Big\{j = 1, \ldots, n : Z_j = z, \;\; \sum_{k: Z_k = z} \mathbf{1}\{\|\widehat{\mathbf{D}}_z(\mathbf{X}_i) - \widehat{\mathbf{D}}_z(\mathbf{X}_k)\| \leq \|\widehat{\mathbf{D}}_z(\mathbf{X}_i) - \widehat{\mathbf{D}}_z(\mathbf{X}_j)\|\} \leq M \Big\},
% \end{align}
% where $\|\cdot\|$ denotes the Euclidean norm on the standardized score space.
The imputed potential outcome $Y_i(z)$ for each individual $i$ is
\begin{align}\label{eq:imputation}
\widehat{Y}_i^{R}(z) =
\begin{cases}
Y_i, & Z_i = z, \\[2mm]
\displaystyle \frac{\sum_{j \in \mathcal{J}_{\mathbf{D}_z}(i)} w_z(\mathbf{X}_j)\, Y_j}{\sum_{j \in \mathcal{J}_{\mathbf{D}_z}(i)} w_z(\mathbf{X}_j)}, & Z_i \neq z,
\end{cases}
\end{align}
where the superscript $R$ denotes retrospective sampling and the survey weights $w_z(\mathbf{X}_j)$ are those of the matched individuals from arm $z$. It is worth noting that in standard unweighted matching, each matched individual contributes equally with weight $1/M$, targeting the sample-level conditional mean or assuming simple random sampling.\cite{abadie2006,yang_multiply_2023} In the survey observational data setting, matched individuals with larger survey weights represent more population individuals, and the survey-weighted imputation in \eqref{eq:imputation} corrects for this by weighting each matched individual proportionally to $w_z(\mathbf{X}_j)$ to target the population-level conditional mean, ensuring that the imputed counterfactual reflects the population composition within each matched set.

Once both potential outcomes $\widehat{Y}_i^{R}(1)$ and $\widehat{Y}_i^{R}(0)$ are obtained for all individuals, the retrospective PATE estimator aggregates the arm-specific imputed means through Hájek normalization,
\begin{align}\label{eq:pate_estimator}
\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{PATE}}^{R} = \widehat{\mu}_1^{R} - \widehat{\mu}_0^{R} = \frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^n w_{Z_i}(\mathbf{X}_i)} \sum_{i=1}^n w_{Z_i}(\mathbf{X}_i) \{\widehat{Y}_i^{R}(1) - \widehat{Y}_i^{R}(0)\},
\end{align}
where $\widehat{\mu}_z^{R} = \sum_{i=1}^n w_{Z_i}(\mathbf{X}_i) \widehat{Y}_i^{R}(z) / \sum_{i=1}^n w_{Z_i}(\mathbf{X}_i)$ estimates each arm-specific potential outcome mean separately.\cite{yang_multiply_2023}

To analyze the asymptotic properties of $\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{PATE}}^{R}$, it is useful to rewrite the estimator in terms of matching weights that express each observed outcome's total contribution. In standard unweighted matching, the reuse structure is summarized by the count $K_M(i) = \sum_{l} \mathbf{1}(i \in \mathcal{J}_{\mathbf{D}_z}(l))$ recording the number of times individual $i$ is used as a match, and the total matching weight on $Y_i$ is $(2Z_i - 1)(1 + M^{-1}K_M(i))$.\cite{abadie2006,yang_multiply_2023} Under survey-weighted matching, equal within-match contributions are replaced by normalized survey weights, and the reuse count generalizes to the survey-weighted reuse frequency: each time individual $i$ in arm $z$ is used as a match for a recipient $l$ in arm $1-z$, individual $i$ contributes its normalized survey weight $w_z(\mathbf{X}_i)/\sum_{k \in \mathcal{J}_{\mathbf{D}_z}(l)} w_z(\mathbf{X}_k)$ to the imputation of $Y_l(z)$, weighted by the recipient's survey weight $w_{1-z}(\mathbf{X}_l)$. For retrospective PATE, the survey-weighted reuse frequency for individual $i$ in arm $z$ is
\begin{align}\label{eq:reuse_freq}
K_z^{R,\mathrm{PATE}}(i)
=
\sum_{l:Z_l=1-z}
w_{1-z}(\mathbf{X}_l)
\frac{w_z(\mathbf{X}_i)\,\mathbf{1}\{i\in\mathcal{J}_{\mathbf{D}_z}(l)\}}
{\sum_{k\in\mathcal{J}_{\mathbf{D}_z}(l)} w_z(\mathbf{X}_k)}, \qquad z \in \{0,1\}.
\end{align}
The arm-specific imputed mean admits the matching weight representation
\begin{align}\label{eq:arm_weight_rep}
\widehat{\mu}_z^{R}
=
\frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^n w_{Z_i}(\mathbf{X}_i)}
\sum_{i:Z_i=z}\{w_z(\mathbf{X}_i)+K_z^{R,\mathrm{PATE}}(i)\}Y_i, \qquad z \in \{0,1\},
\end{align}
and the WDSM estimator can be written compactly as
\begin{align}\label{eq:matching_weight_rep}
\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{PATE}}^{R} = \widehat{\mu}_1^{R} - \widehat{\mu}_0^{R}
=
\frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^n w_{Z_i}(\mathbf{X}_i)}
\sum_{i=1}^n (2Z_i - 1)\{w_{Z_i}(\mathbf{X}_i)+K_{Z_i}^{R,\mathrm{PATE}}(i)\}Y_i.
\end{align}
This matching weight representation parallels the rewrite in Abadie and Imbens\cite{abadie2006} and Yang and Zhang,\cite{yang_multiply_2023} and provides the foundation for the bias decomposition in Section~\ref{subsec:decomposition and bias correction}. The retrospective PATT counterpart is given in Section~\ref{sec:PATT extension}, and the prospective counterparts are collected in Appendix~\ref{app:prospective_pate} and Appendix~\ref{app:prospective_patt}.

\subsection{Bias Decomposition and Correction}\label{subsec:decomposition and bias correction}

Following Abadie and Imbens\cite{abadie2006} and Yang and Zhang,\cite{yang_multiply_2023} we decompose the estimation error of $\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{PATE}}^{R}$ into three components that isolate the sources of variability and bias. Define the conditional treatment effect $\tau(\mathbf{X}_i) = \mu_1(\mathbf{X}_i) - \mu_0(\mathbf{X}_i)$ and the residual $\varepsilon_{z,i} = Y_i - \mu_z(\mathbf{X}_i)$ for individuals in arm $z$. The estimation error decomposes as
\begin{align}\label{eq:bias_decomp}
\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{PATE}}^{R} - \tau_{\mathrm{PATE}}
=
H_{R,\mathrm{PATE}}
+
B_{R,\mathrm{PATE}}
+
E_{R,\mathrm{PATE}},
\end{align}
where
\begin{align}
H_{R,\mathrm{PATE}}
&=
\frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^n w_{Z_i}(\mathbf{X}_i)}
\sum_{i=1}^n w_{Z_i}(\mathbf{X}_i)\{\tau(\mathbf{X}_i) - \tau_{\mathrm{PATE}}\},
\label{eq:H_term}
\\
B_{R,\mathrm{PATE}}
&=
\frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^n w_{Z_i}(\mathbf{X}_i)}
\sum_{i=1}^n
w_{Z_i}(\mathbf{X}_i)(2Z_i - 1)
\left[
\mu_{1-Z_i}(\mathbf{X}_i)
-
\frac{\sum_{j \in \mathcal{J}_{\mathbf{D}_{1-Z_i}}(i)} w_{1-Z_i}(\mathbf{X}_j)\, \mu_{1-Z_i}(\mathbf{X}_j)}
{\sum_{j \in \mathcal{J}_{\mathbf{D}_{1-Z_i}}(i)} w_{1-Z_i}(\mathbf{X}_j)}
\right],
\label{eq:B_term}
\\
E_{R,\mathrm{PATE}}
&=
\frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^n w_{Z_i}(\mathbf{X}_i)}
\sum_{i=1}^n (2Z_i - 1)\{w_{Z_i}(\mathbf{X}_i) + K_{Z_i}^{R,\mathrm{PATE}}(i)\}\,\varepsilon_{Z_i,i}.
\label{eq:E_term}
\end{align}
The term $H_{R,\mathrm{PATE}}$ is a Hájek-type heterogeneity term that captures the survey-weighted deviation of individual treatment effects from the population average, with mean zero by the Hájek recovery identity from Assumption~\ref{ass:retro_ignorability}. The term $B_{R,\mathrm{PATE}}$ is the matching discrepancy that measures the difference between each individual's outcome regression value $\mu_{1-Z_i}(\mathbf{X}_i)$ and its survey-weighted average among matched individuals. Under the two-dimensional score-radius rate and Lipschitz outcome regression regularity (detailed in Web Appendix A), $B_{R,\mathrm{PATE}} = O_p(n^{-1/2})$. The term $E_{R,\mathrm{PATE}}$ aggregates matching-weight-scaled residuals and determines the asymptotic variance.

Although $B_{R,\mathrm{PATE}}$ vanishes at rate $O_p(n^{-1/2})$, removing it can improve finite-sample performance. Following Abadie and Imbens,\cite{AbadieImbens2011} we construct a bias-corrected (BC) imputation by replacing outcomes with residuals and adding back the regression prediction at the target individual's covariate value. Let $\widehat{\mu}_z(\mathbf{D}_z(\mathbf{X}))$ be a nonparametric estimator of $\mu_z(\mathbf{D}_z(\mathbf{X})) = E[Y \mid Z = z, \mathbf{D}_z(\mathbf{X})]$, for example using the method of sieves.\cite{chen2007sieve} For each individual $i$ with $Z_i \neq z$, the BC imputed potential outcome is
\begin{align}\label{eq:bc_imputation}
\widehat{Y}_{i}^{R,\mathrm{BC}}(z)
=
\widehat{\mu}_z(\mathbf{D}_z(\mathbf{X}_i))
+
\frac{1}{\sum_{j \in \mathcal{J}_{\mathbf{D}_z}(i)} w_z(\mathbf{X}_j)}
\sum_{j \in \mathcal{J}_{\mathbf{D}_z}(i)} w_z(\mathbf{X}_j)\{Y_j - \widehat{\mu}_z(\mathbf{D}_z(\mathbf{X}_j))\},
\end{align}
where the superscript BC denotes bias-corrected, and $\widehat{Y}_{i}^{R,\mathrm{BC}}(z) = Y_i$ when $Z_i = z$. The bias-corrected WDSM estimator is
\begin{align}\label{eq:bc_estimator}
\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{PATE}}^{R,\mathrm{BC}}
= \widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{PATE}}^{R} - \widehat{B}_{R,\mathrm{PATE}}
=
\frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^n w_{Z_i}(\mathbf{X}_i)}
\sum_{i=1}^n w_{Z_i}(\mathbf{X}_i) \{\widehat{Y}_i^{R,\mathrm{BC}}(1) - \widehat{Y}_i^{R,\mathrm{BC}}(0)\},
\end{align}
where $\widehat{B}_{R,\mathrm{PATE}}$ is obtained by replacing $\mu_z$ with $\widehat{\mu}_z$ in \eqref{eq:B_term}. The estimation error of the bias-corrected estimator satisfies $\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{PATE}}^{R,\mathrm{BC}} - \tau_{\mathrm{PATE}} = H_{R,\mathrm{PATE}} + E_{R,\mathrm{PATE}}$, with the matching discrepancy $B_{R,\mathrm{PATE}}$ eliminated. The retrospective PATT formulas are given in Section~\ref{sec:PATT extension}, and the prospective analogues are recorded in Appendix~\ref{app:prospective_pate}.

 % The survey weights enter the WDSM point estimator at four stages under retrospective sampling and three under prospective sampling. First, propensity score estimation requires treatment-specific weights $w_z(\mathbf{X}_i)$ under retrospective sampling to recover the population propensity score from the differentially selected sample, but no weights under prospective sampling where the sample and population propensity scores coincide. Second, within-match imputation uses weights $w_{1-z}(\mathbf{X}_j)$ of matched individuals from the opposite treatment arm under retrospective sampling, or common weights $w(\mathbf{X}_j)$ under prospective sampling, to properly weight matched individuals from different sampling strata. Third, Hájek normalization applies treatment-specific weights $w_z(\mathbf{X}_i)$ under retrospective sampling or common weights $w(\mathbf{X}_i)$ under prospective sampling to target the population average treatment effect. Fourth, bias correction estimation incorporates survey weights to ensure consistent estimation of $\widehat{\mu}_z(\mathbf{D}_z)$ through survey-weighted nonparametric regression on the double score. Matching itself uses unweighted Euclidean distance in the double score space, and prognostic scores can be consistently estimated without weights due to sample-level treatment ignorability.

% The survey weights enter the WDSM point estimator at four stages under retrospective sampling and three under prospective sampling. First, propensity score estimation requires treatment-specific weights $w_z(\mathbf{X}_i)$ under retrospective sampling to recover the population propensity score from the differentially selected sample, but no weights under prospective sampling where the sample and population propensity scores coincide. Second, within-match imputation uses weights $w_{1-z}(\mathbf{X}_j)$ of matched individuals from the opposite treatment arm under retrospective sampling, or common weights $w(\mathbf{X}_j)$ under prospective sampling, to properly weight matched individuals from different sampling strata. \subsection{Main Asymptotic Results}\label{subsec:asymptotics}

In this subsection, we establish the asymptotic properties of the bias-corrected WDSM estimator $\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{PATE}}^{R,\mathrm{BC}}$, which depends on the estimated double scores $\widehat{\mathbf{D}}_z(\mathbf{X}_i; \widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}) = (\widehat{e}(\mathbf{X}_i; \widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}), \widehat{\Psi}_z(\mathbf{X}_i; \widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_z))$ with parameters $\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}} = (\widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^\top, \widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_0^\top, \widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_1^\top)^\top$ obtained in Section~\ref{subsec:score_models}. Let $\boldsymbol{\theta}^*$ denote the probability limit of $\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$, which equals the true parameter values when all score models are correctly specified but may differ from them under misspecification. When either the propensity score model is correctly specified, $e(\mathbf{X}; \boldsymbol{\alpha}^*) = e(\mathbf{X})$, or both prognostic score models are correctly specified, $\Psi_z(\mathbf{X}; \boldsymbol{\beta}_z^*) = \Psi_z(\mathbf{X})$ for $z \in \{0,1\}$, $\mathbf{D}_z(\mathbf{X}_i; \boldsymbol{\theta}^*)$ remains a balancing score satisfying $Y_i(z) \perp Z_i \mid \mathbf{D}_z(\mathbf{X}_i; \boldsymbol{\theta}^*)$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:ds_balancing}. We first derive the large sample distribution when $\boldsymbol{\theta}^*$ is treated as known, which we refer to as the oracle setting, and then derive the asymptotic results with variance adjustment from first-step estimation of $\boldsymbol{\theta}^*$.

As noted in Section~\ref{subsec:decomposition and bias correction}, the bias-corrected WDSM estimator has remaining estimation error $\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{PATE}}^{R,\mathrm{BC}} - \tau_{\mathrm{PATE}} = H_{R,\mathrm{PATE}} + E_{R,\mathrm{PATE}}$, where $H_{R,\mathrm{PATE}}$ has mean zero because $E[w_Z(\mathbf{X})\{\tau(\mathbf{X}) - \tau_{\mathrm{PATE}}\} \mid S=1] = 0$ by the definition of $\tau_{\mathrm{PATE}}$, and $E_{R,\mathrm{PATE}}$ has conditional mean zero given $(\mathbf{X}_1, Z_1, \ldots, \mathbf{X}_n, Z_n)$ because $E[\varepsilon_{z,j} \mid \mathbf{X}_j, Z_j = z] = 0$ by the definition of the residual $\varepsilon_{z,j} = Y_j - \mu_z(\mathbf{X}_j)$. Both terms are therefore $O_p(n^{-1/2})$ and $\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{PATE}}^{R,\mathrm{BC}}$ is $\sqrt{n}$-consistent for $\tau_{\mathrm{PATE}}$. 

To derive the asymptotic variance, we further prove in Web Appendix A using the martingale central limit theorem\cite{abadie_large_2006, yang_multiply_2023} that $\sqrt{n}\,H_{R,\mathrm{PATE}}$ and $\sqrt{n}\,E_{R,\mathrm{PATE}}$ are each asymptotically normal with variances $V_{H,R}$ and $V_{E,R}$, respectively, and that $E[H_{R,\mathrm{PATE}} \cdot E_{R,\mathrm{PATE}}] = 0$ by the law of iterated expectations. The oracle asymptotic variance of $\sqrt{n}(\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{PATE}}^{R,\mathrm{BC}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^*) - \tau_{\mathrm{PATE}})$ therefore decomposes additively as $V_R = V_{H,R} + V_{E,R}$. As $n\to\infty$, the heterogeneity variance $V_{H,R}$ of $H_{R,\mathrm{PATE}}$ in Equation \eqref{eq:H_term} takes the form
\begin{align}\label{eq:V_H}
V_{H,R}
&=
\lim_{n\to\infty}
\frac{1}{\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n w_{Z_i}(\mathbf{X}_i)\right)^2}
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n w_{Z_i}^2(\mathbf{X}_i)\{\tau(\mathbf{X}_i)-\tau_{\mathrm{PATE}}\}^2
\nonumber\\
&=
\frac{1}{\left(E[w_{Z}(\mathbf{X}) \mid S = 1]\right)^2}\operatorname{Var}\!\left(w_{Z}(\mathbf{X})\{\tau(\mathbf{X})-\tau_{\mathrm{PATE}}\} \mid S = 1\right).
\end{align}
The residual variance $V_{E,R}$, determined by the matching-weight structure in Equation \eqref{eq:E_term}, generalizes the closed-form limiting variance in Chen and Han\cite{chen2024limiting} from unweighted matching on $d$-dimensional covariates to the survey-weighted double score setting with $d = 2$:
\begin{align}\label{eq:V_E}
V_{E,R}
&=
\lim_{n\to\infty}
\frac{n}{\left(\sum_{i=1}^n w_{Z_i}(\mathbf{X}_i)\right)^2}
\Bigg[
\sum_{j:Z_j=1}\{w_1(\mathbf{X}_j)+K_1^{R,\mathrm{PATE}}(j)\}^2\sigma_1^2(\mathbf{X}_j)
\nonumber\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad
+
\sum_{j:Z_j=0}\{w_0(\mathbf{X}_j)+K_0^{R,\mathrm{PATE}}(j)\}^2\sigma_0^2(\mathbf{X}_j)
\Bigg]
\nonumber\\
&=
\frac{1}{\left(E[w_{Z}(\mathbf{X}) \mid S = 1]\right)^2}
\Bigg[
P(Z = 1 \mid S = 1)\,
E\!\left[\sigma_1^2(\mathbf{X})\,\chi_1^{R}(\mathbf{D}_1(\mathbf{X}))\mid Z = 1,\, S = 1\right]
\nonumber\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad
+
P(Z = 0 \mid S = 1)\,
E\!\left[\sigma_0^2(\mathbf{X})\,\chi_0^{R}(\mathbf{D}_0(\mathbf{X}))\mid Z = 0,\, S = 1\right]
\Bigg],
\end{align}
where $\chi_z^{R}(\mathbf{D}_z(\mathbf{X}))$ is the score-cell probability limit of the squared matching coefficient $\{w_z(\mathbf{X}_j) + K_z^{R,\mathrm{PATE}}(j)\}^2$ conditional on the double score value $\mathbf{D}_z(\mathbf{X})$, whose explicit form extends the geometric constant $\alpha(M,2)$ of Chen and Han\cite{chen2024limiting} to the survey-weighted setting and is derived in Web Appendix A. 
When the scores in $\mathbf{D}_z$ are estimated in the first stage as described in Section~\ref{subsec:score_models}, the oracle variance $V_R$ requires adjustment. Because matching estimators are non-smooth functionals of the matching variables,\cite{abadie2016matching} this adjustment cannot be derived by standard Taylor expansion and instead requires the local experiment framework of Andreou and Werker,\cite{AndreouWerker2012} as adapted by Yang and Zhang\cite{yang_multiply_2023} for double score matching, applied to the stacked estimating function $U(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = (U_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})^\top, U_{\boldsymbol{\beta}_0}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_0)^\top, U_{\boldsymbol{\beta}_1}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_1)^\top)^\top$ from Equations \eqref{eq:ps_ee}--\eqref{eq:pgs_ee}. The following theorem formalizes the asymptotic normality of $\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{PATE}}^{R,\mathrm{BC}}$ under both the oracle and estimated-score settings, with complete proofs given in Web Appendix A.


% The bias-corrected WDSM estimator $\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{PATE}}^{R,\mathrm{BC}}$ has estimation error $\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{PATE}}^{R,\mathrm{BC}} - \tau_{\mathrm{PATE}} = H_{R,\mathrm{PATE}} + E_{R,\mathrm{PATE}}$, where the matching discrepancy is removed by the bias correction term $\widehat{B}_{R,\mathrm{PATE}}$ defined in Equation \eqref{eq:bc_estimator}. As noted in Section~\ref{subsec:decomposition and bias correction}, $H_{R,\mathrm{PATE}}$ has mean zero by the Hájek recovery identity under Assumption~\ref{ass:retro_ignorability}, and $E_{R,\mathrm{PATE}}$ has conditional mean zero given $(\mathbf{X}_1, Z_1, \ldots, \mathbf{X}_n, Z_n)$ because $E[\varepsilon_{z,j} \mid \mathbf{X}_j, Z_j = z] = 0$ by the definition of the residual $\varepsilon_{z,j} = Y_j - \mu_z(\mathbf{X}_j)$. Both terms are therefore $O_p(n^{-1/2})$ and $\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{PATE}}^{R,\mathrm{BC}}$ is $\sqrt{n}$-consistent for $\tau_{\mathrm{PATE}}$. To derive the asymptotic variance, we prove in Web Appendix A that $\sqrt{n}\,H_{R,\mathrm{PATE}}$ and $\sqrt{n}\,E_{R,\mathrm{PATE}}$ are each asymptotically normal with variances $V_{H,R}$ and $V_{E,R}$, respectively. Because $H_{R,\mathrm{PATE}}$ is measurable with respect to $(\mathbf{X}_1, Z_1, \ldots, \mathbf{X}_n, Z_n)$ and $E_{R,\mathrm{PATE}}$ has conditional mean zero given the same sigma-field, the law of iterated expectations gives $E[H_{R,\mathrm{PATE}} \cdot E_{R,\mathrm{PATE}}] = 0$ (Theorem A.6 in Web Appendix A), and the oracle variance decomposes additively as $V_R = V_{H,R} + V_{E,R}$. 
% The residual variance $V_{E,R}$ generalizes the closed-form limiting variance of Chen and Han\cite{chen2024limiting} from unweighted matching on $d$-dimensional covariates to the survey-weighted double score setting with $d = 2$. Because matching estimators are non-smooth functionals of the matching variables,\cite{abadie2016matching} the variance adjustment from first-step score estimation cannot be derived by standard Taylor expansion and instead requires the local experiment framework of Andreou and Werker.\cite{AndreouWerker2012} Let $U(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = (U_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})^\top, U_{\boldsymbol{\beta}_0}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_0)^\top, U_{\boldsymbol{\beta}_1}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_1)^\top)^\top$ denote the stacked estimating function from Equations \eqref{eq:ps_ee}--\eqref{eq:pgs_ee}, where the treatment-specific survey weights $w_{Z_i}(\mathbf{X}_i)$ enter $U_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}$ through Equation \eqref{eq:ps_ee}. The complete proofs for both the oracle and estimated-score results are given in Web Appendix A. The following theorem formalizes these results.



\begin{theorem}[Asymptotic Normality of Retrospective PATE Estimators]
\label{thm:main_asymptotics}
Under Assumptions~\ref{ass:retro_ignorability}, the two-dimensional matching regularity conditions, and the residual stabilization condition in Web Appendix A, if any model of the propensity score or prognostic score is correctly specified:

\textbf{(i) Known scores.} The bias-corrected WDSM estimator satisfies
\begin{align}\label{eq:clt_known}
\sqrt{n}\left(\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{PATE}}^{R,\mathrm{BC}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^*)-\tau_{\mathrm{PATE}}\right)
\Rightarrow
\mathcal{N}\!\left(0,\,V_R\right),
\end{align}
where
\begin{align}\label{eq:V_R}
V_R
&= V_{H,R} + V_{E,R}
\nonumber\\
&=
\frac{1}{\left(E[w_{Z}(\mathbf{X}) \mid S = 1]\right)^2}
\operatorname{Var}\!\left(w_{Z}(\mathbf{X})\{\tau(\mathbf{X})-\tau_{\mathrm{PATE}}\} \mid S = 1\right)
\nonumber\\
&\quad
+
\frac{1}{\left(E[w_{Z}(\mathbf{X}) \mid S = 1]\right)^2}
\Bigg[
P(Z = 1 \mid S = 1)\,
E\!\left[\sigma_1^2(\mathbf{X})\,\chi_1^{R}(\mathbf{D}_1(\mathbf{X}))\mid Z = 1,\, S = 1\right]
\nonumber\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad
+
P(Z = 0 \mid S = 1)\,
E\!\left[\sigma_0^2(\mathbf{X})\,\chi_0^{R}(\mathbf{D}_0(\mathbf{X}))\mid Z = 0,\, S = 1\right]
\Bigg],
\end{align}
and $\chi_z^{R}(\mathbf{D}_z(\mathbf{X}))$ is the score-cell probability limit of $\{w_z(\mathbf{X}_j) + K_z^{R,\mathrm{PATE}}(j)\}^2$ conditional on $\mathbf{D}_z(\mathbf{X})$, as defined in Web Appendix A.

\textbf{(ii) Estimated scores.} Under the additional non-smooth local experiment regularity in Web Appendix A, the approximate distribution of $\sqrt{n}\{\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{PATE}}^{R,\mathrm{BC}}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}})-\tau_{\mathrm{PATE}}\}$ is $\mathcal{N}(0, V_{R,\mathrm{adj}})$, where
\begin{align}\label{eq:V_adj}
V_{R,\mathrm{adj}}
=
V_{R}
-\gamma_{1}^\top\Sigma_U^{-1}\gamma_{1}
+\gamma_{2}^\top\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^*}\gamma_{2},
\end{align}
$\Sigma_U = E[U(\boldsymbol{\theta}^*) U(\boldsymbol{\theta}^*)^\top]$ is the second moment matrix of the stacked estimating function, $\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^*} = \Gamma^{-1}\Sigma_U(\Gamma^{-1})^\top$ is the Godambe sandwich covariance\cite{Binder1983} of $\sqrt{n}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}} - \boldsymbol{\theta}^*)$ with Jacobian $\Gamma = E[\partial U(\boldsymbol{\theta}^*)/\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}^\top]$, $\gamma_1$ governs the asymptotic covariance between the oracle matching statistic and the score estimation influence function, and $\gamma_2$ is the target derivative along the local experiment path, as defined in Web Appendix A.
\end{theorem}

Under simple random sampling where $w_z(\mathbf{x}) = 1$ for all $\mathbf{x} \in \mathcal{X}$ and $z \in \{0,1\}$, $V_R$ reduces to the known-score variance that Yang and Zhang\cite{yang_multiply_2023} derived for unweighted double score matching. 
% As in the unweighted case,\cite{yang_multiply_2023} for a fixed number of matches $M$, $V_R$ does not achieve the semiparametric efficiency bound\cite{Hahn1998} but becomes closer as $M$ increases. 
For part (ii), the variance reduction term $-\gamma_1^\top\Sigma_U^{-1}\gamma_1$ in Equation \eqref{eq:V_adj} is a nonnegative quadratic form arising from the correlation between the matching estimator and the score function for the double score parameters, extending the efficiency gain that Abadie and Imbens\cite{abadie2016matching} established for propensity score matching to the survey-weighted setting. 
The variance inflation term $\gamma_2^\top\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^*}\gamma_2$, on the other hand, arises because if either the propensity score model or the prognostic score model is misspecified, the target estimand may depend on the nuisance parameters through $\tau_{\mathrm{PATE}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^*) = E[\mu_1(\mathbf{D}_1(\mathbf{X}; \boldsymbol{\theta}^*)) - \mu_0(\mathbf{D}_0(\mathbf{X}; \boldsymbol{\theta}^*))]$, as Yang and Zhang\cite{yang_multiply_2023} identified for unweighted double score matching.
The sign of the overall adjustment $-\gamma_1^\top\Sigma_U^{-1}\gamma_1 + \gamma_2^\top\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^*}\gamma_2$ therefore depends on the data generating process, and matching on estimated double scores can either increase, decrease, or maintain estimation efficiency compared to matching on known scores. The retrospective PATT extension is given in Section~\ref{sec:PATT extension}, and the prospective counterparts are presented in Web Appendix B.

\subsection{Variance Estimation and Inference}\label{subsec:variance estimation}


Constructing valid confidence intervals for $\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{PATE}}^{R,\mathrm{BC}}$ requires a consistent variance estimator. Although Theorem \ref{thm:main_asymptotics} characterizes the asymptotic variance explicitly, direct plug-in estimation of $V_R$ and $V_{R,\mathrm{adj}}$ is impractical because the score-cell coefficient $\chi_z^R(\mathbf{D}_z(\mathbf{X}))$ and the covariance vector $\gamma_1$ and $\gamma_2$ do not have tractable closed-form estimators, as Yang and Zhang\cite{yang_multiply_2023} noted for unweighted double score matching. Resampling provides an alternative, but the standard bootstrap is inapplicable because it cannot reproduce the distribution of matching reuse counts for fixed $M$.\cite{abadie2008failure} To overcome this, Otsu and Rai\cite{otsu2017bootstrap} developed a wild bootstrap based on the asymptotic linear representation for unweighted covariate matching, and Yang and Zhang\cite{yang_multiply_2023} extended the linearization-based replication to double score matching by constructing bootstrap replicates from the linearized estimator while retaining the original matching structure. In the survey-weighted setting, an additional challenge is that the variance depends not only on matching reuse frequencies but also on how survey weights are distributed within each match set, as this determines the relative contribution of individuals from different subpopulations to each imputed potential outcome. Extending Yang and Zhang\cite{yang_multiply_2023} to this setting, we develop a linearization-based replication procedure that preserves both the matching reuse structure and the survey weight distribution across replicates.

% \subsubsection{Linearization Framework and Replication Algorithm}\label{sec:linearization for bootstrap}

Combining the heterogeneity term $H_{R,\mathrm{PATE}}$ in Equation \eqref{eq:H_term} and the residual term $E_{R,\mathrm{PATE}}$ in Equation \eqref{eq:E_term}, the estimation error of the bias-corrected estimator admits the asymptotic linear representation
\begin{align}\label{eq:influence_rep}
\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{PATE}}^{R,\mathrm{BC}} - \tau_{\mathrm{PATE}} = \frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^n w_{Z_i}(\mathbf{X}_i)} \sum_{i=1}^n w_{Z_i}(\mathbf{X}_i) \, \varphi_i + o_p(n^{-1/2}),
\end{align}
where
\begin{align}\label{eq:influence_fn}
\varphi_i = \{\tau(\mathbf{X}_i) - \tau_{\mathrm{PATE}}\} + (2Z_i - 1)\frac{w_{Z_i}(\mathbf{X}_i) + K_{Z_i}^{R,\mathrm{PATE}}(i)}{w_{Z_i}(\mathbf{X}_i)}\,\varepsilon_{Z_i,i}
\end{align}
combines the heterogeneity and residual contributions. Based on this linearization, we construct bootstrap replicates of $\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{PATE}}^{R,\mathrm{BC}}$ directly from the linear terms with replicated nuisance parameters, so that the matching reuse structure is retained without re-matching.\cite{yang_multiply_2023} The replication algorithm proceeds as follows.

\begin{description}
\item[Step 1.(Bootstrap Weight Generation)] Generate multinomial weights $\mathbf{m}^* = (m_1^*, \ldots, m_n^*) \sim \text{Multinomial}(n, \mathbf{1}_n/n)$ and construct multiplicative bootstrap weights $w_i^{*,b} = m_i^* \cdot w_{Z_i}(\mathbf{X}_i)$.

\item[Step 2.(Score Parameter Replication)] Obtain a bootstrap replicate $\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*,b}$ of the score parameters by solving the weighted estimating equations with bootstrap weights. For retrospective sampling, the propensity score replicate solves $\sum_{i=1}^n w_i^{*,b} \mathbf{X}_i \{Z_i - e(\mathbf{X}_i; \boldsymbol{\alpha})\} = 0$, while for prospective sampling the unweighted multinomial weights $m_i^*$ replace $w_i^{*,b}$, consistent with the design-specific score estimation in Section \ref{subsec:score_models}. For both designs, prognostic score replicates solve $\sum_{i: Z_i=z} m_i^* \mathbf{X}_i \{Y_i - \Psi_z(\mathbf{X}_i; \boldsymbol{\beta}_z)\} = 0$. Apply polynomial sieve bias correction\cite{chen2007sieve} with the bootstrap double scores to obtain $\widehat{\mu}_z^{\mathrm{BC},*,b}(\mathbf{D}_z)$ for $z \in \{0,1\}$.

\item[Step 3.(Linearized Bootstrap Estimate)] Compute a bootstrap replicate of $\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{PATE}}^{R,\mathrm{BC}}$ as
\begin{align}\label{eq:boot_tau}
\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{PATE}}^{*,b} = \frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^n w_i^{*,b}} \sum_{i=1}^n \Big[&w_i^{*,b}\{\widehat{\mu}_1^{\mathrm{BC},*,b}(\mathbf{D}_{1,i}) - \widehat{\mu}_0^{\mathrm{BC},*,b}(\mathbf{D}_{0,i})\} \notag\\
&+ m_i^*(2Z_i - 1)\{w_{Z_i}(\mathbf{X}_i) + K_{Z_i}^{R,\mathrm{PATE}}(i)\}\{Y_i - \widehat{\mu}_{Z_i}^{\mathrm{BC},*,b}(\mathbf{D}_{Z_i,i})\}\Big],
\end{align}
where the survey-weighted reuse frequencies $K_{Z_i}^{R,\mathrm{PATE}}(i)$ are held fixed from the original matching structure.

\item[Step 4.(Variance Calculation)] Repeat Steps 1--3 for $b = 1, \ldots, B$ replicates and estimate the variance by $\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{PATE}}^{R,\mathrm{BC}}) = B^{-1}\sum_{b=1}^B (\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{PATE}}^{*,b} - \bar{\tau}^*)^2$, with $\bar{\tau}^* = B^{-1}\sum_{b=1}^B \widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{PATE}}^{*,b}$.
\end{description}

The variance estimation rows of Table \ref{tab:weight_requirements} summarize the survey weight requirements for each component of this procedure.

\section{Extension to Population Average Treatment Effect on the Treated}\label{sec:PATT extension}

Estimation of the PATT requires only one-sided matching from treated to control individuals on the control-side double score $\mathbf{D}_0(\mathbf{X}) = (e(\mathbf{X}), \Psi_0(\mathbf{X}))$, which satisfies $Y(0) \perp Z \mid \mathbf{D}_0(\mathbf{X})$ by Lemma \ref{lem:ds_balancing} under the one-sided identification condition $Y(0) \perp Z \mid \mathbf{X}$. Define the conditional treatment effect among the treated $\tau_{\mathrm{PATT}}(\mathbf{X}) = \mu_{1\mid 1}(\mathbf{X}) - \mu_0(\mathbf{X})$, where $\mu_{1\mid 1}(\mathbf{X}) = E[Y(1) \mid \mathbf{X}, Z=1]$, and the treated-side residual $\varepsilon_{1\mid 1,i} = Y_i - \mu_{1\mid 1}(\mathbf{X}_i)$. 




For each treated individual $i$, the potential outcome $Y_i(1) = Y_i$ is directly observed and the counterfactual control outcome is imputed from $M$ nearest controls in the $\mathbf{D}_0$ space:
\begin{align}\label{eq:patt_imputation}
\widehat{Y}_i^{R}(0)
=
\frac{1}{\sum_{k\in \mathcal{J}_{\mathbf{D}_0}(i)} w_0(\mathbf{X}_k)}
\sum_{j\in \mathcal{J}_{\mathbf{D}_0}(i)}
w_0(\mathbf{X}_j) Y_j,
\qquad
Z_i=1.
\end{align}
Control individuals may appear in multiple treated match sets, and the survey-weighted reuse frequency for the control arm is defined as
\begin{align}\label{eq:patt_reuse_freq}
K_0^{R,\mathrm{PATT}}(j)
=
\sum_{i:Z_i=1}
w_1(\mathbf{X}_i)
\frac{w_0(\mathbf{X}_j)\mathbf{1}\{j\in\mathcal{J}_{\mathbf{D}_0}(i)\}}{\sum_{k\in\mathcal{J}_{\mathbf{D}_0}(i)} w_0(\mathbf{X}_k)}.
\end{align}
With these components, the retrospective PATT estimator uses Hájek normalization with treated-side survey weights and has the matching weight representation
\begin{align}\label{eq:patt_estimator}
\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{PATT}}^{R}
&=
\frac{1}{\sum_{i:Z_i=1} w_1(\mathbf{X}_i)}
\sum_{i:Z_i=1} w_1(\mathbf{X}_i)\{Y_i - \widehat{Y}_i^{R}(0)\}
\nonumber\\
&=
\frac{1}{\sum_{i:Z_i=1} w_1(\mathbf{X}_i)}
\left[
\sum_{i:Z_i=1} w_1(\mathbf{X}_i) Y_i - \sum_{j:Z_j=0} K_0^{R,\mathrm{PATT}}(j) Y_j
\right].
\end{align}

For the asymptotic properties, the estimation error $\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{PATT}}^{R} - \tau_{\mathrm{PATT}}$ decomposes into the same three-term structure as in Section \ref{subsec:decomposition and bias correction}, $H_{R,\mathrm{PATT}} + B_{R,\mathrm{PATT}} + E_{R,\mathrm{PATT}}$, with explicit formulas given in Web Appendix A. The bias-corrected estimator $\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{PATT}}^{R,\mathrm{BC}} = \widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{PATT}}^R - \widehat{B}_{R,\mathrm{PATT}}$ removes the $O_p(n^{-1/2})$ matching discrepancy, where $\widehat{B}_{R,\mathrm{PATT}}$ is constructed by replacing $\mu_0$ with $\widehat{\mu}_0(\mathbf{D}_0(\mathbf{X}))$ in $B_{R,\mathrm{PATT}}$, paralleling Equation \eqref{eq:bc_estimator}. The following theorem establishes the asymptotic properties of $\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{PATT}}^{R,\mathrm{BC}}$.

\begin{theorem}[Asymptotic Normality of Retrospective PATT Estimators]
\label{thm:patt_asymptotics}
Under the conditions of Theorem \ref{thm:main_asymptotics} with the one-sided identification condition $Y(0) \perp Z \mid \mathbf{X}$:

\textbf{(i) Known scores.} The bias-corrected PATT estimator satisfies
\begin{align}\label{eq:patt_clt_known}
\sqrt{n}\left(\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{PATT}}^{R,\mathrm{BC}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^*) - \tau_{\mathrm{PATT}}\right)
\Rightarrow
\mathcal{N}\!\left(0,\, V_{R,\mathrm{PATT}}\right),
\end{align}
where
\begin{align}\label{eq:V_PATT_oracle}
V_{R,\mathrm{PATT}}
&= V_{H,R,\mathrm{PATT}} + V_{E,R,\mathrm{PATT}}
\nonumber\\
&=
\frac{1}{\left(E[Z w_1(\mathbf{X}) \mid S = 1]\right)^2}
\operatorname{Var}\!\left(Z w_1(\mathbf{X})\{\tau_{\mathrm{PATT}}(\mathbf{X}) - \tau_{\mathrm{PATT}}\} \mid S = 1\right)
\nonumber\\
&\quad
+
\frac{1}{\left(E[Z w_1(\mathbf{X}) \mid S = 1]\right)^2}
\Bigg[
P(Z = 1 \mid S = 1)\,
E\!\left[w_1^2(\mathbf{X})\,\sigma_{1\mid 1}^2(\mathbf{X})\mid Z = 1,\, S = 1\right]
\nonumber\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad
+
P(Z = 0 \mid S = 1)\,
E\!\left[\sigma_0^2(\mathbf{X})\,\kappa_{0,2}^{R,\mathrm{PATT}}(\mathbf{D}_0(\mathbf{X}))\mid Z = 0,\, S = 1\right]
\Bigg],
\end{align}
and $\kappa_{0,2}^{R,\mathrm{PATT}}(\mathbf{D}_0(\mathbf{X}))$ is the score-cell probability limit of $\{K_0^{R,\mathrm{PATT}}(j)\}^2$ conditional on $\mathbf{D}_0(\mathbf{X})$, as defined in Web Appendix A.

\textbf{(ii) Estimated scores.} Under the additional non-smooth local experiment regularity in Web Appendix A, the approximate distribution of $\sqrt{n}\{\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{PATT}}^{R,\mathrm{BC}}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}) - \tau_{\mathrm{PATT}}\}$ is $\mathcal{N}(0, V_{R,\mathrm{PATT},\mathrm{adj}})$, where
\begin{align}\label{eq:V_PATT_adj}
V_{R,\mathrm{PATT},\mathrm{adj}}
=
V_{R,\mathrm{PATT}}
- \gamma_1^\top\Sigma_U^{-1}\gamma_1
+ \gamma_2^\top\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^*}\gamma_2,
\end{align}
with $\Sigma_U$, $\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^*}$ defined as in Theorem \ref{thm:main_asymptotics}, and $\gamma_1$, $\gamma_2$ defined in Web Appendix A.
\end{theorem}

For variance estimation of $\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{PATT}}^{R,\mathrm{BC}}$, the linearization-based replication procedure of Section \ref{subsec:variance estimation} applies with two modifications: only the control-side bias correction $\widehat{\mu}_0^{\mathrm{BC},*,b}(\mathbf{D}_0)$ is needed, and the reuse structure involves only $K_0^{R,\mathrm{PATT}}(j)$ with Hájek normalization restricted to treated individuals. The corresponding asymptotic linear representation is
\begin{align}\label{eq:patt_influence_rep}
\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{PATT}}^{R,\mathrm{BC}} - \tau_{\mathrm{PATT}} = \frac{1}{\sum_{i:Z_i=1} w_1(\mathbf{X}_i)} \sum_{i=1}^n \varphi_i^{\mathrm{PATT}} + o_p(n^{-1/2}),
\end{align}
where $\varphi_i^{\mathrm{PATT}} = w_1(\mathbf{X}_i)\{\tau_{\mathrm{PATT}}(\mathbf{X}_i) - \tau_{\mathrm{PATT}} + \varepsilon_{1\mid 1,i}\}$ for $Z_i = 1$ and $\varphi_j^{\mathrm{PATT}} = -K_0^{R,\mathrm{PATT}}(j)\varepsilon_{0,j}$ for $Z_j = 0$, leading to the bootstrap replicate
\begin{align}\label{eq:patt_boot_tau}
\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{PATT}}^{*,b} = \frac{1}{\sum_{i:Z_i=1} w_i^{*,b}}
\left[
\sum_{i:Z_i=1} w_i^{*,b} \{Y_i - \widehat{\mu}_0^{\mathrm{BC},*,b}(\mathbf{D}_{0,i})\}
-
\sum_{j:Z_j=0} m_j^* K_0^{R,\mathrm{PATT}}(j) \{Y_j - \widehat{\mu}_0^{\mathrm{BC},*,b}(\mathbf{D}_{0,j})\}
\right],
\end{align}
where $K_0^{R,\mathrm{PATT}}(j)$ is held fixed from the original matching structure. The variance estimate is $\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{PATT}}^{R,\mathrm{BC}}) = B^{-1}\sum_{b=1}^B (\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{PATT}}^{*,b} - \bar{\tau}_{\mathrm{PATT}}^*)^2$. The prospective PATT analogue is presented in Web Appendix B.

% % % \begin{theorem}[Asymptotic Normality of Retrospective PATT Estimators]
% % % \label{thm:patt_asymptotics}
% % % Under the sampling and one-sided PATT identification conditions of Section~\ref{sec: notation and assumption}, the shared two-dimensional matching regularity, and the matching-weight residual stabilization condition:

% % % \textbf{(i) Known scores.} The uncorrected PATT estimator satisfies
% % % \begin{align}
% % % \sqrt n\left(\widehat\tau_{\mathrm{PATT}}^{R}-\tau_{\mathrm{PATT}}-B_{R,\mathrm{PATT}}\right)
% % % &\Rightarrow
% % % N\!\left(0,V_{R,\mathrm{PATT},\mathrm{oracle}}\right),
% % % \end{align}
% % % where $V_{R,\mathrm{PATT},\mathrm{oracle}}=V_{H,R,\mathrm{PATT}}+V_{E,R,\mathrm{PATT}}$. If the bias-corrected estimator removes $B_{R,\mathrm{PATT}}$ at $o_p(n^{-1/2})$ rate, the same limit holds without the centering term.

% % % \textbf{(ii) Estimated scores.} Under Assumptions A1--A4 and the same non-smooth local-experiment regularity, if any model of the propensity score or prognostic score is correctly specified, the approximate distribution of $n^{1/2}\{\widehat\tau_{\mathrm{PATT}}^{R,\mathrm{BC}}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_R)-\tau_{\mathrm{PATT}}\}$ is $\mathcal{N}(0, V_{R,\mathrm{PATT}})$, where
% % % \begin{align}\label{eq:V_PATT}
% % % V_{R,\mathrm{PATT}}
% % % &=
% % % V_{R,\mathrm{PATT},\mathrm{oracle}}
% % % -\gamma_{R,\mathrm{PATT},1}^\top\Sigma_{R,\mathrm{PATT},U}^{-1}\gamma_{R,\mathrm{PATT},1}
% % % +\gamma_{R,\mathrm{PATT},2}^\top\Sigma_{R,\mathrm{PATT},\theta}\gamma_{R,\mathrm{PATT},2},
% % % \end{align}
% % % where the sandwich objects $(\Gamma_{R,\mathrm{PATT}}, \Sigma_{R,\mathrm{PATT},U}, \Sigma_{R,\mathrm{PATT},\theta})$ are defined as in Theorem~\ref{thm:main_asymptotics}, and $\gamma_{R,\mathrm{PATT},1}$ and $\gamma_{R,\mathrm{PATT},2}$ are given in Web Appendix A. Under fixed law, $\gamma_{R,\mathrm{PATT},2}=0$ and the last term vanishes.
% % % \end{theorem}

% % % Unlike PATE, where $\gamma_{R,\mathrm{PATE},2}=0$ because the population average treatment effect does not depend on the propensity score parameters, the PATT variance inflation term $\gamma_{R,\mathrm{PATT},2}^\top\Sigma_{R,\mathrm{PATT},\theta}\gamma_{R,\mathrm{PATT},2}$ is generally nonzero because $\tau_{\mathrm{PATT}} = E[\tau(\mathbf{X})e_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{X})]/E[e_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{X})]$ depends on the propensity score parameters through the treated subpopulation weighting. Consequently, the sign of the overall adjustment $V_{R,\mathrm{PATT}} - V_{R,\mathrm{PATT},\mathrm{oracle}}$ depends on the data-generating process, and ignoring the estimation error in the double score may lead to confidence intervals that are either too large or too small, paralleling the finding of Abadie and Imbens\cite{abadie2016matching} for ATET estimation with propensity score matching. Under the default one-sided identification route, $\gamma_{R,\mathrm{PATT},2}$ is carried abstractly unless additional path structure is imposed. The prospective PATT analogue is recorded in Appendix~\ref{app:prospective_patt}.

% % % \subsection{Variance Estimation for PATT}

% % % The linearization-based bootstrap procedure for PATT follows the same structure as for PATE (Section~\ref{sec:linearization for bootstrap}), with two modifications reflecting the one-sided matching structure. First, only the control-side bias correction $\widehat{\mu}_0^{bc,*,b}(\mathbf{D}_0)$ is needed, since the treated potential outcome $Y_i(1)$ is directly observed for treated individuals. Second, the matching reuse structure involves only the control-side matching frequency $K_0^{R,\mathrm{PATT}}(j)$, with the Hájek normalization restricted to treated individuals.

% % % After bias correction, the PATT estimator admits the influence function representation
% % % \begin{align}
% % % \widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{PATT}}^{R,\mathrm{BC}} - \tau_{\mathrm{PATT}} = \frac{1}{\sum_{i:Z_i=1} w_1(\mathbf{X}_i)} \sum_{i=1}^n \varphi_i^{\mathrm{PATT}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^*) + o_p(n^{-1/2}),
% % % \end{align}
% % % where for treated individuals ($Z_i=1$), $\varphi_i^{\mathrm{PATT}} = w_1(\mathbf{X}_i)[\tau_T(\mathbf{X}_i) - \tau_{\mathrm{PATT}} + \varepsilon_{1\mid 1,i}]$, and for control individuals ($Z_j=0$), $\varphi_j^{\mathrm{PATT}} = -K_0^{R,\mathrm{PATT}}(j)\varepsilon_{0,j}$. The bootstrap replicate for PATT is
% % % \begin{align}
% % % \widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{PATT}}^{*,b} = \frac{1}{\sum_{i:Z_i=1} w_i^{*,b}} \sum_{i:Z_i=1} w_i^{*,b} \Big[Y_i &- \widehat{\mu}_0^{bc,*,b}(\mathbf{D}_{0,i}) \Big] \notag\\
% % % &- \frac{1}{\sum_{i:Z_i=1} w_i^{*,b}} \sum_{j:Z_j=0} m_j^* K_0^{R,\mathrm{PATT}}(j) [Y_j - \widehat{\mu}_0^{bc,*,b}(\mathbf{D}_{0,j})],
% % % \end{align}
% % % where the control-side matching frequencies $K_0^{R,\mathrm{PATT}}(j)$ are held fixed from the original matching structure. The variance estimate is $\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{PATT}}^{R,\mathrm{BC}}) = B^{-1}\sum_{b=1}^B (\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{PATT}}^{*,b} - \bar{\tau}_{\mathrm{PATT}}^*)^2$. The large-sample targets are $V_{R,\mathrm{PATT}}$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:patt_asymptotics}). The prospective PATT analogue is recorded in Appendix~\ref{app:prospective_patt}.






\section{Simulation Studies}\label{sec:simulation}

\subsection{Simulation design}

Our data-generating process builds upon the framework in Zeng et al.,\cite{zeng2025movingbestpracticeusing} which adapted the multi-stage strategy from Austin\cite{Austin2016} and Lenis et al.\cite{Lenis2019} We simulated a population of $1{,}000{,}000$ individuals, organized into 10 strata $(j = 1, \ldots, 10)$. Each stratum was further divided into 20 clusters, resulting in a total of 200 clusters indexed globally as $(k = 1, \ldots, 200)$, with 5000 individuals in each cluster $(i = 1, \ldots, 5000)$. For each individual, we generated six baseline covariates $(l = 1, \ldots, 6)$ from normal distributions, where the vector of covariates for the $i$-th individual is denoted as $\mathbf{X}_i = (X_{1i}, X_{2i}, X_{3i}, X_{4i}, X_{5i}, X_{6i})^\top$. The means of these covariates were allowed to vary at both the stratum and cluster levels. For the $l$-th covariate, a stratum-specific random effect for the $j$ th stratum $\nu_{l, j}^{\text {stratum }} \sim N\left(0, \sigma_l^{\text {stratum }}\right)$ was generated, along with a cluster-specific random effect for the $k$ -th cluster $\nu_{l, k}^{\text {cluster }} \sim N\left(0, \sigma_l^{\text {cluster }}\right)$. 
The $l$-th covariate for the $i$-th individual in the $j$-th stratum and $k$ -th cluster was then generated as $X_{l, i j k} \sim N\left(\nu_{l, j}^{\text {stratum }}+\nu_{l, k}^{\text {cluster }}, 1\right)$. This hierarchical structure builds in correlation within clusters (through shared stratum and cluster effects) as well as within strata (through shared stratum effects only), while maintaining independence between strata. We set $\sigma^{\text{stratum}} = 0.35$ and $\sigma^{\text{cluster}} = 0.15$ throughout.
We specified the true population-level propensity score for each individual using a logistic model:
\begin{align}
\operatorname{logit}(e(\mathbf{X}_i)) = a_0 + \psi(\boldsymbol{a}_{1:6}^\top \mathbf{X}_i + a_7 X_{1i} X_{2i}), \nonumber
\end{align}
where $\boldsymbol{a}_{1:6} = (\log(1.1), \log(1.25), \log(1.5), \log(1.75), \log(2), \log(2.5))^\top$ and $a_7 = \log(1.1)$. Treatment assignment $Z_i$ was simulated from a Bernoulli distribution: $Z_i \sim \operatorname{Bernoulli}(e(\mathbf{X}_i))$. The scalar $\psi$ reflects the strength of confounding and dictates the level of overlap in the propensity score.\cite{Hu2021} We set $\psi = 0.6$ or $2$ to mimic scenarios with good or poor overlap in the full population. For both scenarios, the overall treatment prevalence was maintained at approximately 30\% by adjusting $a_0$ to $\log(35/80)$ for good overlap and $\log(20/80)$ for poor overlap, allowing us to isolate the impact of overlap when evaluating estimator performance.

We simulated continuous outcomes for each individual $i$ in the population using a linear combination of covariates, treatment effects, and interaction terms, with
\begin{align}
Y_i = b_0 + \delta_0(\boldsymbol{b}^\top \mathbf{X}_i + b_7 X_{1i} X_{2i}) + Z_i[\delta_1 + \delta_2(\boldsymbol{b}^\top \mathbf{X}_i + b_8 X_{1i} X_{2i})] + \varepsilon_i, \nonumber
\end{align}
where $\varepsilon_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$. We set $b_0 = 0$ and $\boldsymbol{b} = (2.5, -2, 1.75, -1.25, 1.5, 1.1)^\top$. The average treatment effect was represented by $\delta_1 = 1$, while $\delta_0 = 0.3$ and $\delta_2 = 0.2$ introduced heterogeneity in the control and treatment conditions, respectively. Two interaction terms were included, with $b_7 = 2.5$ and $b_8 = 1.5$, to capture the more complex influence of baseline covariates on outcomes across both conditions.

We drew 1,000 random samples under two sampling frameworks. Under retrospective sampling (our primary focus), the sampling mechanism depended on both baseline covariates and treatment assignment. The sampling probability $\pi_z(\mathbf{x}_i) = P(S_i = 1 \mid Z_i, \mathbf{X}_i)$ was computed as
\begin{align}
\operatorname{logit}(\pi_z(\mathbf{x}_i)) = c_0 + \delta_z^{(s)} Z_i + \mathbf{c}^\top \mathbf{X}_i, \nonumber
\end{align}
where $c_0 = \log(0.005/0.995)$ defines the baseline sampling rate of 0.5\%, and $\delta_z^{(s)} = \log(0.9)$ slightly reduces the sampling probability for the treatment group. The coefficient vector was specified as $\mathbf{c} = (\log(1.05), \log(1.10), \log(1.15), \log(1.10), \log(1.05), \log(1.10))^\top$. Treatment-specific survey weights were computed as $w_z(\mathbf{x}_i) = 1/\pi_z(\mathbf{x}_i)$. Under prospective sampling, samples of 5000 individuals each (representing 0.5\% of the overall population) were drawn using a multi-stage design where the sampling mechanism was independent of the treatment assignment mechanism. Sample sizes were allocated across the 10 strata as $\{850, 750, 700, 650, 600, 400, 350, 300, 250, 150\}$, ensuring that the observed sample would have a different distribution from the population. Within each stratum, five clusters were selected using simple random sampling, and from each selected cluster, an equal number of individuals were sampled. For each sampled individual, we calculated the true survey weights as $w_i = 1/\pi(\mathbf{x}_i)$ by taking the inverse of the multi-stage inclusion probability.

For each sample within each scenario, we compared five matching methods: unweighted propensity score matching (PSM), unweighted prognostic score matching (PGM), unweighted double score matching (DSM), survey-weighted propensity score matching (SW-PSM) via MatchIt,\cite{Ho2011} and the proposed weighted double score matching (WDSM). All matching used $M = 5$ nearest neighbors with replacement in standardized score space, with full results for $M \in \{1, 3, 5\}$ presented in Web Appendix C. WDSM incorporated polynomial sieve bias correction and the linearization-based bootstrap variance estimation of Section \ref{subsec:variance estimation} with $B = 200$ replications. We assessed performance under four model misspecification scenarios: (i) correct propensity score model and correct prognostic score model (Cor|Cor), (ii) misspecified propensity score model but correct prognostic score model (Mis|Cor), (iii) correct propensity score model but misspecified prognostic score model (Cor|Mis), and (iv) both models misspecified (Mis|Mis). Correctly specified models included all six main effects plus the $X_{1i}X_{2i}$ interaction, while misspecified models omitted this interaction. This misspecification design parallels Yang and Zhang,\cite{yang_multiply_2023} who evaluated standard unweighted double score matching under similar scenarios.

Given the complexity of the design, we note the total number of scenarios considered. We examined two levels of overlap (good versus poor), two sampling designs (prospective versus retrospective), five matching methods (PSM, PGM, DSM, SW-PSM, WDSM), two estimands (PATE, PATT), and four model specification scenarios (Cor|Cor, Mis|Cor, Cor|Mis, Mis|Mis). This combination yields $2 \times 2 \times 5 \times 2 \times 4 = 160$ distinct configurations in the main analysis. Additional scenarios with varying $M$ increase this number further. Table \ref{tab:scenario_counts} outlines these factors and their levels for quick reference.

Our simulation framework adapts key elements from \cite{zeng2025movingbestpracticeusing} while focusing specifically on the challenges of matching estimators under complex survey designs.

% \subsection{Simulation design}


% Our data-generating process builds upon the framework in \cite{zeng2025movingbestpracticeusing}, which was adapted the multi-stage strategy from \cite{Austin2016} and \cite{Lenis2019}. 
% We simulated a population of $1{,}000{,}000$ individuals, organized into 10 strata $(j = 1, \ldots, 10)$. Each stratum was further divided into 20 clusters, resulting in a total of 200 clusters indexed globally as $(k = 1, \ldots, 200)$, with 5000 individuals in each cluster $(i = 1, \ldots, 5000)$. For each individual, we generated six baseline covariates $(l = 1, \ldots, 6)$ from normal distributions, where the vector of covariates for the $i$-th individual is denoted as $\mathbf{X}_i = (X_{1i}, X_{2i}, X_{3i}, X_{4i}, X_{5i}, X_{6i})^T$. The means of these covariates were allowed to vary at both the stratum and cluster levels. For the $l$-th covariate, a stratum-specific random effect for the $j$-th stratum $\nu_{l,j}^{\text{stratum}} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^{\text{stratum}})$ was generated, along with a cluster-specific random effect for the $k$-th cluster $\nu_{l,k}^{\text{cluster}} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^{\text{cluster}})$. The $l$-th covariate for the $i$-th individual in the $j$-th stratum and $k$-th cluster was then generated as $X_{l,ijk} \sim \mathcal{N}(\nu_{l,j}^{\text{stratum}} + \nu_{l,k}^{\text{cluster}}, 1)$. This hierarchical structure builds in correlation within clusters (through shared stratum and cluster effects) as well as within strata (through shared stratum effects only), while maintaining independence between strata. We set $\sigma^{\text{stratum}} = 0.35$ and $\sigma^{\text{cluster}} = 0.15$ throughout.

% We specified the true population-level propensity score for each individual using a logistic model:
% \begin{align}
% \operatorname{logit}(e(\mathbf{X}_i)) = a_0 + \psi(\boldsymbol{a}_{1:6}^T \mathbf{X}_i + a_7 X_{1i} X_{2i}), \nonumber
% \end{align}
% where $\boldsymbol{a}_{1:6} = (\log(1.1), \log(1.25), \log(1.5), \log(1.75), \log(2), \log(2.5))^T$ and $a_7 = \log(1.1)$. Treatment assignment $Z_i$ was simulated from a Bernoulli distribution: $Z_i \sim \operatorname{Bernoulli}(e(\mathbf{X}_i))$. The scalar $\psi$ reflects the strength of confounding and dictates the level of overlap in the propensity score.\cite{Hu2021} We set $\psi = 0.6$ or $2$ to mimic scenarios with good or poor overlap in the full population. For both scenarios, the overall treatment prevalence was maintained at approximately 30\% by adjusting $a_0$ to $\log(35/80)$ for good overlap and $\log(20/80)$ for poor overlap, allowing us to isolate the impact of overlap when evaluating estimator performance.

% We simulated continuous outcomes for each individual $i$ in the population using a linear combination of covariates, treatment effects, and interaction terms, with
% \begin{align}
% Y_i = b_0 + \delta_0(\boldsymbol{b}^T \mathbf{X}_i + b_7 X_{1i} X_{2i}) + Z_i[\delta_1 + \delta_2(\boldsymbol{b}^T \mathbf{X}_i + b_8 X_{1i} X_{2i})] + \varepsilon_i, \nonumber
% \end{align}
% where $\varepsilon_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$. We set $b_0 = 0$ and $\boldsymbol{b} = (2.5, -2, 1.75, -1.25, 1.5, 1.1)^T$. The average treatment effect was represented by $\delta_1 = 1$, while $\delta_0 = 0.3$ and $\delta_2 = 0.2$ introduced heterogeneity in the control and treatment conditions, respectively. Additionally, two interaction terms were included, with $b_7 = 2.5$ and $b_8 = 1.5$, to capture the more complex influence of baseline covariates on outcomes across both conditions.

% We drew 1,000 random samples under two sampling frameworks. Under prospective sampling, samples of 5000 individuals each (representing 0.5\% of the overall population) were drawn using a multi-stage design where the sampling mechanism was independent of the treatment assignment mechanism. Sample sizes were allocated across the 10 strata as $\{850, 750, 700, 650, 600, 400, 350, 300, 250, 150\}$, ensuring that the observed sample would have a different distribution from the population. Within each stratum, five clusters were selected using simple random sampling, and from each selected cluster, an equal number of individuals were sampled. For each sampled individual, we calculated the true survey weights as $w_i = 1/\pi(\mathbf{x}_i)$ by taking the inverse of the multi-stage inclusion probability.

% To further assess the performance of our estimators under retrospective designs (our primary focus), we introduced a sampling mechanism that depends on both baseline covariates and treatment assignment. We used logistic regression to model the sampling probability for individual $i$ as a function of both the treatment assignment indicator $Z_i$ and covariates $\mathbf{X}_i$, allowing sampling to reflect more complex real-world dependencies. The sampling probability $\pi_z(\mathbf{x}_i) = P(S_i = 1 \mid Z_i, \mathbf{X}_i)$ was computed as
% \begin{align}
% \operatorname{logit}(\pi_z(\mathbf{x}_i)) = c_0 + \delta_z^{(s)} Z_i + \mathbf{c}^T \mathbf{X}_i, \nonumber
% \end{align}
% where $c_0 = \log(0.005/0.995)$ defines the baseline sampling rate of 0.5\% consistent with the above multi-stage design, and $\delta_z^{(s)} = \log(0.9)$ slightly reduces the sampling probability for the treatment group. The coefficient vector was specified as $\mathbf{c} = (\log(1.05), \log(1.10), \log(1.15), \log(1.10), \log(1.05), \log(1.10))^T$. The sampling indicator $S_i$ was subsequently generated as a Bernoulli random variable with probability $\pi_z(\mathbf{x}_i)$, capturing the interaction between treatment assignment and covariates in determining sample inclusion. Treatment-specific survey weights were computed as $w_z(\mathbf{x}_i) = 1/\pi_z(\mathbf{x}_i)$.

% For each sample within each scenario, we compared five matching methods: unweighted propensity score matching (PSM), unweighted prognostic score matching (PGM), unweighted double score matching (DSM), survey-weighted propensity score matching (SW-PSM) via MatchIt,\cite{Ho2011} and the proposed weighted double score matching (WDSM). All matching used $M = 5$ nearest neighbors with replacement in standardized score space, with results for $M \in \{1, 3\}$ presented in Web Appendix. \subsection{Simulation results}

We focus on three key questions to evaluate WDSM performance: (1) What are the consequences of ignoring survey weights in double score matching? (2) How does WDSM compare to survey-weighted propensity score matching? (3) Which method provides greater robustness under model misspecification and varying matching ratios? We primarily examine PATE under retrospective sampling, then briefly discuss extensions to PATT and prospective designs. 
Figure \ref{fig:retro_overlap_comparison} presents the simulation results under retrospective sampling, comparing bias, relative efficiency, and coverage across five matching methods for PATE and PATT estimation under good overlap (top panels) and poor overlap (bottom panels). Detailed numerical results are provided in Web Tables 1 (good overlap) and 2 (poor overlap). Prospective sampling results are presented in Web Figure 1 and Web Tables 3 and 4, and results for varying matching ratios $M \in \{1, 3\}$ in Web Tables 5 and 6. To facilitate the summary of findings, we focus on the following three questions, that is, whether survey weight incorporation is necessary for consistent matching estimation under retrospective sampling, how WDSM compares to existing survey-weighted and unweighted matching methods, and which method provides greater robustness under model misspecification and poor overlap.


\subsubsection{Is survey weight incorporation necessary for matching under retrospective sampling?}

The simulation results confirm that incorporating survey weights in both the score estimation and outcome analysis steps is essential for consistent estimation of population-level treatment effects using matching. As shown in Figure \ref{fig:retro_overlap_comparison} (top-left panel), all three unweighted methods (PSM, PGM, DSM) exhibit 5--7\% relative bias for PATE even when both models are correctly specified (Cor|Cor), with PGM and DSM achieving below-nominal coverage at 83.0\% and 86.0\% (top-right panel). In contrast, WDSM achieves near-zero bias with 94.0\% coverage for PATE and 96.0\% coverage for PATT under the same scenario. This pattern persists across all model specification scenarios, confirming that the bias of unweighted methods arises from the unaccounted sampling mechanism rather than model misspecification.

Under poor overlap (bottom panels of Figure \ref{fig:retro_overlap_comparison}), the consequences of ignoring survey weights become more pronounced. Unweighted PSM bias escalates to 37.6\% for PATE when the propensity score model is misspecified (Mis|Cor), with coverage dropping to 77.0\%. Even unweighted DSM, which achieves double robustness under simple random sampling, shows persistent 5--6\% bias under correct specification due to the unaccounted informative sampling. WDSM reduces this to near-zero bias with 94.0\% coverage (Web Table 2). These results confirm that bias from ignoring survey weights cannot be resolved by improving model specification alone, and that weight incorporation throughout the matching pipeline is essential for valid population-level inference under retrospective sampling.


\subsubsection{How does WDSM compare to existing matching methods?}

When both models are correctly specified (Cor|Cor), WDSM and survey-weighted PSM (SW-PSM) both achieve near-zero bias as visible in Figure \ref{fig:retro_overlap_comparison} (top-left panel), but WDSM provides more reliable interval estimation, achieving 94.0\% coverage for PATE compared to 92.0\% for SW-PSM, and 96.0\% versus 95.0\% for PATT (Web Table 1). The efficiency gains are comparable under good overlap, with both methods achieving approximately twice the efficiency of unweighted PSM. Under poor overlap (bottom-middle panel), the efficiency advantage of WDSM becomes substantially larger: WDSM achieves relative efficiency of 7.04 for PATE and 11.01 for PATT compared to 7.30 and 7.91 for SW-PSM, reflecting the additional information provided by the prognostic score in improving match quality when treatment groups are poorly separated.

The key distinction between WDSM and SW-PSM emerges under model misspecification. Under Mis|Cor with good overlap, SW-PSM relies entirely on the misspecified propensity score and shows 0.11\% bias with 93.0\% coverage for PATE, while WDSM leverages the correctly specified prognostic score to achieve comparable performance with 92.0\% coverage. Under Cor|Mis, where the prognostic score is misspecified but the propensity score is correct, WDSM maintains near-zero bias with 94.4\% coverage for PATE (Web Table 1), confirming that a correct propensity score alone suffices for consistent estimation. This pattern is consistent with the double robustness property established in Theorem \ref{thm:main_asymptotics}, where consistency requires only one correctly specified score model.

Under poor overlap with Mis|Cor, WDSM achieves near-zero bias with 93.0\% coverage for PATE and 94.0\% coverage for PATT (Web Table 2). Unweighted DSM, by contrast, shows 17.8\% bias with 60.0\% coverage for PATT in this scenario (bottom-left panel of Figure \ref{fig:retro_overlap_comparison}), while WDSM maintains near-nominal performance, confirming that double robustness under simple random sampling does not transfer to informative sampling without proper weight incorporation.


\subsubsection{Which methods are more robust under model misspecification and poor overlap?}

When model specifications deviate from the truth, the performance of matching estimators varies by estimand and overlap condition. Under good overlap, WDSM maintains near-zero bias and near-nominal coverage for PATE whenever at least one model is correctly specified, as visible in the top panels of Figure \ref{fig:retro_overlap_comparison}: $-0.56$\% bias with 92.0\% coverage under Mis|Cor, and $-0.004$\% bias with 94.4\% coverage under Cor|Mis (Web Table 1). For PATT, WDSM performs well under Mis|Cor ($-0.42$\% bias, 96.0\% coverage) but shows greater sensitivity to prognostic score misspecification under Cor|Mis (4.05\% bias, 89.0\% coverage), reflecting the one-sided matching structure where PATT relies on control-side prognostic modeling.

Under the most challenging scenario where both models are misspecified (Mis|Mis), all methods experience increased bias and deteriorated coverage. Under good overlap, WDSM shows 4.70\% bias with 91.0\% coverage for PATE, outperforming both unweighted DSM (10.44\% bias, 73.0\% coverage) and SW-PSM (7.05\% bias, 87.0\% coverage). For PATT under Mis|Mis, SW-PSM achieves $-0.60$\% bias with 96.0\% coverage, reflecting that PATT estimation requires only control-side modeling and SW-PSM benefits from the propensity score model not entering the PATT estimand directly. WDSM shows 5.29\% bias with 82.0\% coverage for PATT in this scenario (Web Table 1).

Under poor overlap with Mis|Mis (bottom panels of Figure \ref{fig:retro_overlap_comparison}), WDSM shows increased sensitivity to joint misspecification: PATE bias escalates to 28.3\% with coverage dropping to 53.0\%. SW-PSM maintains 0.2\% bias with 92.0\% coverage in this specific scenario, though its performance advantage is confined to the Mis|Mis condition where prognostic score misspecification amplifies matching errors in the poorly separated covariate space. Across all other specification scenarios under poor overlap, WDSM consistently outperforms SW-PSM in both bias and coverage. These findings motivate careful model specification in practice, particularly for the prognostic score model when overlap is poor, and suggest that supplementary diagnostics such as covariate balance assessment after matching are advisable.

In summary, WDSM achieves near-zero bias and nominal coverage whenever at least one score model is correctly specified, with substantial efficiency gains under poor overlap from the prognostic score. Survey weight incorporation is essential under retrospective sampling, and WDSM provides more reliable inference than SW-PSM across most scenarios. The primary limitation occurs under joint model misspecification with poor overlap, a condition under which all double-score methods are expected to degrade, and which motivates careful model specification and diagnostic assessment in practice.

\section{Case Studies}\label{sec:case studies}

\subsection{Application to Early Marijuana Use and Mental Health Outcomes}

\subsubsection{Data and Research Question}

We illustrate WDSM using data from the 2023 National Survey on Drug Use and Health (NSDUH),\cite{SAMHSA2024} an annual survey conducted by the Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration that provides nationally representative estimates of substance use and mental health among the U.S. civilian, noninstitutionalized population. The NSDUH employs a complex multistage probability sampling design with deliberate oversampling of young adults (18--29 year-olds) and specific demographic groups,\cite{SAMHSA2024codebook} and constitutes a prospective (non-informative) sampling design in our framework.

Our analytic sample consists of 17,471 young adults aged 18--29 after complete case analysis, weighted to represent approximately 51.8 million individuals. Treatment is defined as marijuana use initiation before age 15 ($n=1{,}877$, 9.3\% weighted) versus first use at age 15 or later or never used ($n=15{,}594$, 90.7\% weighted). This age threshold reflects developmental research identifying early adolescence as a critical period for substance use vulnerability and brain development.\cite{Squeglia2009,Lubman2015,Volkow2016effects,Albaugh2021} The endocannabinoid system reaches peak expression and activity throughout the brain during adolescence,\cite{Hurd2019} and cannabis exposure during this neurodevelopmental window may disrupt normal brain maturation processes.\cite{Filbey2014}

The primary outcome is any mental illness in the past year (AMIPY), including anxiety disorders, major depressive episodes, and other mood disorders assessed through structured diagnostic criteria based on DSM-5.\cite{SAMHSA2024} The secondary outcome is serious mental illness (SMIPY), indicating disorders with substantial functional impairment. Prior research has established associations between adolescent cannabis use and increased risk of depression, anxiety, and suicidal ideation in young adulthood.\cite{Gobbi2019,NationalAcademies2017,Volkow2016effects} Confounding variables include demographics (gender, race/ethnicity, age groups), socioeconomic factors (income levels), geographic indicators (metropolitan status), and other substance use patterns (early alcohol and cigarette use).

We implement WDSM following Sections \ref{subsec:decomposition and bias correction} and \ref{subsec:variance estimation} with survey-weighted logistic regression for propensity scores and survey-weighted linear regression for prognostic scores. Matching uses $M=5$ nearest neighbors with replacement in the standardized double score space, with bootstrap variance estimation using $B=200$ replications. For comparison, we implement unweighted propensity score matching (PSM), prognostic score matching (PGM), and double score matching (DSM) with 1:1 matching ratio and bootstrap inference, and survey-weighted propensity score full matching (SW-PS Full) with cluster-robust standard errors.\cite{Ho2011}

\subsubsection{Results}

Figure \ref{fig:casestudy_pate}(a) presents the estimated population average treatment effects of early marijuana use on mental health outcomes. For the primary outcome (AMIPY), WDSM estimates that early marijuana use increases adult mental illness prevalence by 6.68 percentage points (95\% CI: $-0.14$, 13.51), representing a 21\% relative increase from the control group baseline of 32.2\%. This effect is marginally significant ($p=0.055$). The unweighted methods (PSM, PGM, DSM) produce larger point estimates ranging from 8.49 to 9.97 percentage points with confidence intervals excluding zero, consistent with the simulation finding that ignoring survey weights leads to biased estimation. Survey-weighted PS full matching yields a smaller estimate of 4.34 percentage points with substantially wider confidence intervals (95\% CI: $-5.33$, 14.00), reflecting efficiency loss from not leveraging prognostic information. WDSM provides an intermediate point estimate with narrower confidence intervals than SW-PS Full matching, balancing proper survey weight incorporation with the efficiency gains of double score matching.

For serious mental illness (SMIPY), all methods show smaller effects near the null. WDSM estimates a 1.87 percentage point increase (95\% CI: $-1.39$, 5.14), while unweighted methods range from 1.59 to 2.00 percentage points. SW-PS Full matching produces a small negative estimate ($-0.64$ percentage points). The consistency of positive effects across matching methods that incorporate both scores supports the direction of the association.

By properly incorporating survey weights throughout the matching pipeline, WDSM provides more credible population-level estimates than unweighted methods and better efficiency than standard survey-weighted matching. The estimated 6.68 percentage point increase in mental illness prevalence, if causal, represents approximately 308,000 excess cases among U.S. young adults, underscoring the public health relevance of this application.

\subsection{Application to Sleep Duration and Glycemic Control in NHANES}

\subsubsection{Data and Research Question}

We apply WDSM to investigate whether insufficient sleep duration affects glycemic regulation in non-diabetic adults using NHANES 2007--2018 data. With approximately one-third of U.S. adults habitually sleeping less than the recommended 7 hours per night,\cite{liu2016prevalence} understanding sleep's metabolic consequences has substantial implications for diabetes prevention. Laboratory studies demonstrate that experimental sleep restriction impairs glucose tolerance and insulin sensitivity,\cite{spiegel1999impact,buxton2010sleep} but epidemiological evidence for population-level effects remains limited by residual confounding and sample selection issues. The NHANES employs a retrospective (informative) sampling design in our framework, where the treatment-specific survey weights $w_z(\mathbf{x}_i)$ are required for valid population-level inference as established in Section \ref{subsec:survey_design}.

Our analytic sample comprises 3,793 non-diabetic adults aged 40--70 with complete data on self-reported sleep duration and laboratory-measured HbA1c. We dichotomize usual weekday sleep at the clinical threshold of 7 hours,\cite{watson2015recommended} comparing short sleepers ($<7$ hours) to those obtaining adequate sleep ($\geq 7$ hours). The outcome is HbA1c measured as percentage via standardized methods,\cite{little2001national} with values between 5.7\% and 6.4\% indicating prediabetes.\cite{ada2024diagnosis} Confounding adjustment includes age, sex, race/ethnicity, education, poverty-income ratio, BMI, and smoking status. We implement WDSM with $M=1, 3, 5$ matches to examine the bias-variance tradeoff, while unweighted methods use 1:1 matching. All matching estimators employ bootstrap inference with $B=200$ replications.

\subsubsection{Results}

Figure \ref{fig:casestudy_pate}(b) presents estimated effects of short sleep duration on HbA1c levels. The unweighted methods yield small positive but statistically non-significant estimates ranging from 0.040\% to 0.055\%, and survey-weighted PS full matching similarly does not detect a significant effect (0.047\%, 95\% CI: $-0.012$\%, 0.105\%) despite properly accounting for complex sampling. In contrast, all three WDSM specifications produce larger point estimates ranging from 0.080\% to 0.101\%, with WDSM ($M=3$) and WDSM ($M=5$) yielding confidence intervals excluding zero.

The progression from $M=1$ to $M=5$ illustrates the bias-variance tradeoff: point estimates decrease modestly as additional matches introduce small bias, but confidence intervals narrow substantially due to variance reduction, consistent with the asymptotic theory in Theorem \ref{thm:main_asymptotics}. WDSM with $M=5$ achieves 52\% narrower confidence intervals than PS full matching while maintaining statistical significance. The difference between the WDSM population-weighted estimates (0.080--0.101\%) and the average unweighted estimate (0.049\%) represents approximately 60--100\% upward adjustment when survey weights are properly incorporated, suggesting that the complex sampling design of NHANES creates meaningful sample-population differences in the sleep-glycemic relationship.

While the WDSM estimates are modest in absolute terms, they represent measurable subclinical metabolic impairment at the population level. Extrapolated to the approximately 85 million U.S. adults who habitually sleep less than 7 hours,\cite{liu2016prevalence} even this small per-person effect translates to considerable population burden of prediabetes risk. The contrast between the significant WDSM findings and the null results from other methods illustrates how ignoring survey weights or relying on single-score matching can attenuate true associations, particularly for behavioral exposures that correlate with survey design features.

\section{Discussion and Conclusion}\label{sec:discussion}

This paper develops weighted double score matching (WDSM) for population-level causal inference from complex survey data. The framework incorporates survey weights throughout the matching pipeline, including score estimation, within-match averaging, Hájek normalization, and bias correction, under both retrospective and prospective sampling designs. We establish asymptotic normality for both known and estimated scores (Theorems \ref{thm:main_asymptotics} and \ref{thm:patt_asymptotics}), demonstrate that WDSM preserves double robustness under survey-weighted estimation, and provide a computationally efficient linearization-based bootstrap procedure for inference that avoids re-matching in each replicate. All proposed methods are implemented in the publicly available \texttt{wdsmatch} R package.

Several lessons have emerged from our investigation. First, incorporating survey weights throughout all phases of the matching estimator is essential for consistent estimation of population-level treatment effects in survey observational studies. Consistent with the findings of Ridgeway et al.\cite{Ridgeway2015} and Zeng et al.\cite{zeng2025movingbestpracticeusing} for propensity score weighting, our simulation results confirm that omitting survey weights restricts covariate balance to within the sample,\cite{Zanutto2006,DuGoff2014} introducing persistent bias when generalizing to the target population. Under retrospective sampling where $e_{\text{sp}}(\mathbf{X}) \neq e(\mathbf{X})$, survey-weighted propensity score estimation is necessary for recovering the population propensity score as established in Section \ref{subsec:score_models}. Under prospective sampling where these quantities coincide, either approach is theoretically valid, but incorporating survey weights remains advisable as a safeguard against potential violations of the conditional independence between sampling and treatment.\cite{DuGoff2014}

Second, the use of both propensity and prognostic scores in the matching variable provides substantial advantages over single-score matching. When the propensity score model is misspecified, the prognostic score preserves consistency through double robustness, and vice versa. The efficiency gains from double score matching are especially pronounced under poor overlap, where WDSM achieves relative efficiency of 7.04 for PATE and 11.01 for PATT compared to 7.30 and 7.91 for survey-weighted propensity score matching. This reflects the additional information that the prognostic score provides for improving match quality when treatment groups are poorly separated in the covariate space, consistent with the advantage of double score matching over single-score matching observed by Yang and Zhang\cite{yang_multiply_2023} under simple random sampling. The primary limitation of double score matching relative to single-score propensity matching emerges under joint model misspecification with poor overlap, where misspecification of the prognostic score amplifies matching errors.

Third, practical implementation of WDSM requires attention to several diagnostic and modeling considerations. Individual covariates may remain imbalanced despite overall double score balance, and we recommend post-matching balance assessment using standardized mean differences\cite{Austin2009balance} to verify adequate covariate balance within the matched sample. When residual imbalance is detected, post-matching regression adjustment with survey weights\cite{abadie2016matching} or caliper restrictions on the matching distance can mitigate the impact. The choice of matching ratio $M$ presents a bias-variance tradeoff: our simulations suggest $M = 3$ as a robust default, providing efficiency gains of 20--50\% over 1:1 matching while limiting bias amplification under model misspecification, consistent with the recommendations of Austin.\cite{Austin2009}

Our case studies illustrate the practical consequences of methodological choice. In the NSDUH analysis of early marijuana use and mental health, unweighted DSM overestimates the effect at 8.49 percentage points while survey-weighted propensity score matching underestimates at 4.34 with substantially wider confidence intervals, whereas WDSM provides a balanced estimate of 6.68 percentage points with better precision. In the NHANES analysis of sleep duration and glycemic control, WDSM is the only method to detect a statistically significant effect of short sleep on HbA1c, with all three WDSM specifications ($M=1,3,5$) producing confidence intervals excluding zero while unweighted methods and survey-weighted propensity score matching yield non-significant results. These contrasting findings across prospective and retrospective designs confirm that both ignoring survey weights and relying solely on propensity score matching can lead to misleading conclusions.

The validity of our findings rests on several key assumptions. Like all matching methods, WDSM cannot address unmeasured confounding, with the additional complexity that survey design itself may relate to unmeasured factors affecting treatment and outcomes.\cite{rosenbaum1987sensitivity,vanderweele2017sensitivity} Sensitivity analysis methods for assessing robustness to unmeasured confounding, such as the E-value framework of VanderWeele and Ding\cite{VanderWeele2017} and the flexible sensitivity analysis of Lu and Ding,\cite{LuDing2024} have been developed for weighting and regression estimators but have not been extended to the survey-weighted matching setting. Developing sensitivity analysis tools for WDSM represents an important direction for future research. Our analysis also assumes that survey weights are known and derived from well-designed surveys. If this assumption is violated through inadequate non-response adjustment or flawed post-stratification, the resulting survey weights may exacerbate rather than correct selection bias.

Several extensions of WDSM merit further development. Current limitations to binary treatments motivate extensions to multiple treatments building on the generalized propensity score framework,\cite{yang2020propensity,Li2019b} survival outcomes,\cite{AustinSmall2014} and heterogeneous treatment effects.\cite{imai2013estimating} Each extension requires careful integration of survey weights with specific methodological requirements. Our analysis relies on parametric models for propensity score and prognostic score estimation, reflecting their widespread use in applied practice. Data-adaptive machine learning approaches offer greater flexibility in capturing nonlinear relationships and complex interactions, and extending WDSM to incorporate double machine learning estimators\cite{chernozhukov2018double} for score estimation in the survey-weighted setting represents a promising direction. Computational complexity $O(n^2 M)$ may necessitate approximate algorithms for very large surveys, though efficiency gains from proper weight incorporation typically justify the computational cost for surveys of moderate size.















% \section{Discussion and Conclusion} \label{sec:discussion}

% This paper develops Weighted Double Score Matching (WDSM) for population-level causal inference from complex survey data. The framework incorporates survey weights throughout the matching pipeline, including score estimation, within-match averaging, Hájek normalization, and bias correction, under both retrospective and prospective sampling designs. We establish asymptotic normality for both known and estimated scores (Theorems~\ref{thm:main_asymptotics} and \ref{thm:patt_asymptotics}), demonstrating that WDSM preserves double robustness under survey-weighted estimation, and we provide a computationally efficient linearization-based bootstrap procedure for inference that avoids re-matching in each replicate.

% Our simulations reveal that survey weights are essential for consistency rather than merely variance adjustment. Under retrospective sampling, unweighted methods exhibit 5--10\% bias even with correct specification, while WDSM achieves near-zero bias through proper weight incorporation at all stages. The use of both propensity and prognostic scores is especially valuable when one working model is misspecified, where survey-weighted propensity score matching can fail badly but WDSM remains substantially more stable in bias and coverage. The matching ratio $M$ exhibits different bias-variance trade-offs than unweighted matching, with efficiency gains of 20--50\% from increasing $M$ offset by amplified bias under model misspecification, suggesting $M=3$ as a robust default \citep{Austin2009}.

% Practical implementation requires careful consideration of several issues. Individual covariates may remain imbalanced despite overall double score balance, motivating either direct inclusion of critical confounders in the matching metric or post-matching regression adjustment with survey weights \citep{abadie2016matching}. Our case studies illustrate the practical stakes of methodological choice. In the NSDUH analysis of early marijuana use and mental health, unweighted DSM overestimates the effect at 8.49 percentage points while survey-weighted propensity score matching underestimates at 4.34 with substantially wider confidence intervals, whereas WDSM provides a balanced estimate of 6.68 percentage points with superior precision. In the NHANES analysis of sleep duration and glycemic control, WDSM is the only method to detect a statistically significant effect of short sleep on HbA1c, with all three WDSM specifications ($M=1,3,5$) producing confidence intervals excluding zero, while unweighted methods and survey-weighted propensity score matching all yield non-significant results. These contrasting findings across prospective and retrospective designs demonstrate that both ignoring survey weights and relying solely on propensity score matching can produce misleading conclusions.

The divergence in conclusions across methods in our applications demonstrates that ignoring survey design can fundamentally mislead policy decisions. As surveys remain primary data sources for policy research, methods that properly handle both confounding and complex sampling become essential for credible evidence generation \citep{ridgeway2015propensity,zeng2025movingbestpracticeusing}. By establishing theoretical foundations, demonstrating empirical advantages, and providing practical implementation through the \texttt{wdsmatch} R package, WDSM enhances our ability to leverage survey data for causal insights that reliably inform policy and practice.
