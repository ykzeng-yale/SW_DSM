{\centerline{\sc ABSTRACT}}

\noindent Matching methods are widely used for causal inference from observational data, creating comparable treatment and control groups through systematic pairing of units with similar characteristics. Double score matching has emerged as a robust approach utilizing both propensity and prognostic scores to achieve dimension reduction and protection against model misspecification. However, existing double score matching methods assume simple random sampling and cannot accommodate complex survey designs, limiting their applicability to population-level inference from survey data where sampling probabilities vary systematically across population subgroups.
We develop weighted double score matching (WDSM) estimators that extend double score matching to complex survey designs while preserving doubly robust properties. Under both prospective and retrospective sampling frameworks, we establish comprehensive theoretical foundations including consistency, $\sqrt{n}$-convergence rates, and asymptotic normality for population average treatment effects (PATE) and population average treatment effects on the treated (PATT). We demonstrate that WDSM maintains doubly robust properties—remaining consistent when either propensity or prognostic score models are correctly specified—while enabling valid population-level inference through proper survey weight incorporation. We provide explicit bias decomposition, develop bias correction procedures for finite-sample enhancement, and establish that PATT estimation requires only weaker unconfoundedness assumptions though optimal convergence depends on control group size.
We derive comprehensive variance estimation procedures including analytical sandwich estimators and bootstrap methods that respect survey design complexity and matching uncertainty. Through extensive simulation studies, we demonstrate that WDSM outperforms existing survey-weighted methods, remaining stable under extreme propensity scores while achieving superior finite-sample performance compared to standard approaches. We illustrate the practical utility of WDSM through an empirical application using National Survey on Drug Use and Health data to estimate the causal effect of early marijuana use on adult mental health outcomes. Our findings provide evidence-based guidance for practitioners conducting population-level causal inference from complex survey data using matching methods.

\section{Introduction}

Observational studies serve as a cornerstone of empirical research across disciplines, especially when randomized experiments are impractical, ethically problematic, or resource-prohibitive. Researchers frequently rely on observational data collected through surveys employing sophisticated sampling methodologies intended to ensure sample representativeness of target populations. Large-scale surveys typically implement intricate sampling features including multistage designs, geographic stratification, cluster sampling, and purposeful oversampling of underrepresented demographic groups. Survey weights derived from these sampling protocols are essential for reconstructing population characteristics, properly accounting for differential selection probabilities and patterns of survey nonparticipation.

The design and timing of survey data collection fundamentally shapes the nature of survey weights and their role in causal inference. In prospective sampling designs, selection probabilities depend only on baseline covariates unrelated to treatment assignment, with survey weights reflecting predetermined demographic or geographic characteristics. Conversely, retrospective sampling designs involve selection probabilities that depend on the treatment or exposure variable itself—such as racial health disparities studies where sampling deliberately varies by race, or case-control studies that oversample based on disease status. To estimate valid causal effects from such data requires methods that simultaneously address confounding bias through covariate adjustment and selection bias through appropriate survey weight incorporation. Traditional regression approaches struggle with high-dimensional covariate spaces and model specification sensitivity, motivating the development of balancing score methodologies that achieve dimension reduction while maintaining properties necessary for unbiased treatment effect estimation.



Matching methods have evolved as a fundamental approach to causal inference, creating comparable treatment and control groups through systematic pairing of units with similar characteristics. Early developments relied on exact matching or Mahalanobis distance matching directly on observed covariates (Rubin, 1973), but proved computationally intensive and suffered from the curse of dimensionality as covariate spaces expanded. Rosenbaum and Rubin (1983) revolutionized the field by demonstrating that matching on scalar propensity scores preserves covariate balance while achieving computational tractability and dimension reduction. Subsequent innovations addressed matching optimization and flexibility: optimal matching algorithms minimized global distance rather than greedy sequential decisions (Rosenbaum, 1989; Stuart, 2010); genetic matching automated distance metric selection to optimize balance (Diamond and Sekhon, 2013); coarsened exact matching provided finite-sample balance guarantees through discretization (Iacus et al., 2011). Abadie and Imbens (2006, 2011, 2016) established comprehensive asymptotic theory including bias correction procedures addressing finite-sample performance and variance estimation accounting for matching uncertainty. Recent work has expanded to optimal frameworks balancing bias-variance trade-offs (Kallus, 2020) and examined fundamental questions about matching objectives in replicating experimental designs.


Survey weight incorporation has been actively integrated across causal inference methods, with extensive emperical resutls and theoretical frameworks for stratification (Zanutto, 2006), subclassification (Zanutto et al., 2005), and weighting approaches (DuGoff et al. 2013; Ridgeway et al., 2015; Zeng et al., 2025). Ridgeway et al. (2015) provided theoretical justification for survey-weighted propensity score estimation in weighting methods, while Zeng et al. (2025) developed a unified balancing weights framework establishing comprehensive theoretical foundations for various target estimands under both prospective and retrospective sampling designs. However, matching methods lack comparable theoretical development despite extensive empirical investigation, with fundamental disputes persisting over optimal survey weight utilization strategies. Core disagreements center on two implementation stages: first, whether survey weights should be incorporated during propensity score estimation through survey-weighted models (Ridgeway et al., 2015) versus inclusion as auxiliary covariates (DuGoff et al., 2014); second, whether matched units should retain original survey weights versus adopting transferred weights from their matched counterparts (Lenis et al., 2019). Austin et al. (2018) demonstrated through comprehensive simulation that neither propensity score estimation approach consistently dominates across scenarios, while Lenis et al. (2019) found that survey weight incorporation during score estimation provides limited population-level benefits when adequate covariate balance is achievable, though benefits emerge under specific missingness mechanisms. These disputes reflect deeper methodological tensions between sampling-based perspectives emphasizing population representativeness and experimental design perspectives prioritizing confounding control.


Recently, double score matching has emerged through systematic methodological advances addressing model uncertainty in causal inference. Hansen (2008) established foundational theory by demonstrating that prognostic scores—conditional expectations of potential outcomes—serve as balancing scores complementary to propensity scores for dimension reduction in confounding control. Leacy and Stuart (2014) conducted the first comprehensive simulation study examining joint use of propensity and prognostic scores, comparing three approaches including full matching on Mahalanobis distance combining both scores, demonstrating empirical benefits but lacking formal theoretical foundations. Antonelli et al. (2018) provided the first rigorous theoretical framework for doubly robust matching estimators in high-dimensional settings, proving that estimators combining propensity and prognostic scores maintain consistency when either model is correctly specified while establishing convergence rates superior to single-score approaches. Yang and Zhang (2022) formalized double score matching as a comprehensive theoretical framework, establishing complete asymptotic theory including consistency guarantees, distributional properties, and extending to multiply robust properties accommodating multiple candidate models for enhanced protection against misspecification. Zhang et al. (2022) addressed the critical gap between theory and practice by providing comprehensive implementation guidance including variable selection strategies, caliper choices, and replacement decisions through extensive simulation studies. Extensions have broadened applicability to multilevel treatment settings (Zhao et al., 2022) and quantile treatment effects. This methodological progression has established DSM as a mature approach offering theoretical robustness against model misspecification and superior empirical performance in challenging settings.


Despite comprehensive theoretical development and demonstrated empirical advantages, existing DSM methodology has not addressed survey weight integration, creating a significant gap for population-level causal inference from complex survey data. This limitation prevents legitimate application of DSM to survey-based studies requiring population generalizability, as standard DSM assumes simple random sampling that conflicts with complex survey designs. The technical challenges involve extending DSM's doubly robust properties while accommodating survey selection mechanisms and their interactions with treatment assignment and outcome prediction processes, developing practical algorithms for survey-weighted score estimation and matching procedures, and constructing bias correction and variance estimation methods that respect survey design features while maintaining DSM's efficiency advantages.

This article develops weighted double score matching (WDSM) estimators that extend DSM to complex survey designs while preserving doubly robust properties and computational efficiency. We establish comprehensive theoretical foundations encompassing consistency guarantees, convergence rates, and asymptotic distributions under both prospective and retrospective sampling frameworks for population average treatment effects (PATE) and treatment effects on the treated (PATT). Our contributions include practical bias correction and variance estimation procedures accommodating both matching uncertainty and survey design complexity, comprehensive simulation evidence documenting superior performance relative to existing survey-weighted methods while maintaining robustness advantages, and empirical applications demonstrating practical importance for population-level causal inference from survey data.


The rest of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 develops the methodological framework including notation, identification assumptions, and survey-weighted double score construction. Sections 3 and 4 present WDSM estimators for population average treatment effects and treatment effects on the treated with complete asymptotic theory. Section 5 addresses variance estimation and inference procedures. Section 6 reports extensive simulation studies, Section 7 demonstrates empirical applications, and Section 8 concludes with methodological extensions and future research directions.


















\section{Method}

This section establishes the theoretical foundation for weighted double score matching (WDSM) in complex survey data. We develop a unified framework that extends the double score matching methodology of Yang and Zhang (2020) to incorporate survey weights following the principles established by Zeng et al. (2025), while building upon the rigorous asymptotic theory framework of Abadie and Imbens (2016).

\subsection{Causal Inference Setup and Basic Notation}

We consider a finite population of size $N$ indexed by $i = 1, \ldots, N$. For each unit $i$, we observe covariates $\mathbf{X}_i \in \mathcal{X} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^d$, treatment indicator $Z_i \in \{0,1\}$, and outcome $Y_i \in \mathbb{R}$. Following the potential outcomes framework of Rubin (1974), each unit has potential outcomes $Y_i(0)$ and $Y_i(1)$ corresponding to control and treatment conditions, respectively, with the observed outcome given by $Y_i = Z_i Y_i(1) + (1-Z_i) Y_i(0)$.

Let $S_i \in \{0,1\}$ denote the survey selection indicator, where $S_i = 1$ if unit $i$ is included in the survey sample and $S_i = 0$ otherwise. The survey sample size is $n = \sum_{i=1}^N S_i$, with treatment-specific sample sizes $n_1 = \sum_{i=1}^N S_i Z_i$ and $n_0 = \sum_{i=1}^N S_i (1-Z_i)$.

Our primary estimands are population-level causal parameters. The \textit{population average treatment effect} (PATE) is defined as
\begin{align}
\tau_{\text{PATE}} = E_{\Omega}[Y_i(1) - Y_i(0)],
\end{align}
where $E_{\Omega}[\cdot]$ denotes expectation over the finite population $\Omega$. The \textit{population average treatment effect on the treated} (PATT) is
\begin{align}
\tau_{\text{PATT}} = E_{\Omega}[Y_i(1) - Y_i(0) \mid Z_i = 1].
\end{align}

These population-level estimands differ from their sample analogs and require careful incorporation of survey weights for consistent estimation, as emphasized by Imbens (2004) and Ridgeway et al. (2015).

The fundamental causal identification assumptions follow the standard framework. We assume \textit{consistency}: $Y_i = Z_i Y_i(1) + (1-Z_i) Y_i(0)$, \textit{treatment unconfoundedness}: $(Y_i(0), Y_i(1)) \perp Z_i \mid \mathbf{X}_i$, and \textit{treatment positivity}: $0 < P(Z_i = 1 \mid \mathbf{X}_i) < 1$ almost surely. For PATT estimation, unconfoundedness can be relaxed to require only $Y_i(0) \perp Z_i \mid \mathbf{X}_i$, following Yang and Zhang (2020).

\subsection{Survey Design Frameworks and Weight Structures}

We consider two primary survey design frameworks that differ in the relationship between treatment assignment and survey selection, following the taxonomy of Zeng et al. (2025). A key theoretical insight is that both frameworks utilize population-level matching variables while differing in their independence structures and weighting schemes.

\subsubsection{Prospective Sampling Design}

In prospective sampling, survey selection depends only on pre-treatment covariates, with selection probabilities $p_i = P(S_i = 1 \mid \mathbf{X}_i)$. This implies $P(S_i = 1 \mid \mathbf{X}_i, Z_i) = P(S_i = 1 \mid \mathbf{X}_i) = p_i$, ensuring prospective ignorability when the survey design occurs before treatment assignment. The corresponding survey weights are $w_i = 1/p_i$, representing the inverse probability of selection. This design is common in longitudinal studies where baseline covariates determine sampling probabilities before treatment assignment occurs.

\begin{assumption}[Prospective Survey Ignorability]
\label{ass:prosp_ignorability}
Under prospective sampling, $(Y_i(0), Y_i(1)) \perp S_i \mid \mathbf{X}_i$.
\end{assumption}

\begin{assumption}[Prospective Survey Positivity]
\label{ass:prosp_positivity}
$P(S_i = 1 \mid \mathbf{X}_i) \geq \delta > 0$ almost surely for some constant $\delta$.
\end{assumption}

The prospective design ensures that the survey selection mechanism does not introduce additional confounding beyond that already controlled by $\mathbf{X}_i$. Under this design, sampling and treatment assignment exhibit extended balancing properties: conditional on population-level matching variables $V_{z,i}^{\text{pop}}$, we have both $(Y_i(0), Y_i(1)) \perp Z_i \mid V_{z,i}^{\text{pop}}$ and $S_i \perp Z_i \mid V_{z,i}^{\text{pop}}$, providing a joint conditional independence structure.

\subsubsection{Retrospective Sampling Design}

In retrospective sampling, survey selection probabilities depend on both covariates and treatment status: $p_{z,i} = P(S_i = 1 \mid \mathbf{X}_i, Z_i = z)$ for $z \in \{0,1\}$. This leads to treatment-specific survey weights $w_{z,i} = 1/p_{z,i}$. Retrospective designs are prevalent in case-control studies and post-treatment surveys where treatment status influences the probability of survey participation.

\begin{assumption}[Retrospective Survey Ignorability]
\label{ass:retro_ignorability}
Under retrospective sampling, $(Y_i(0), Y_i(1)) \perp S_i \mid \mathbf{X}_i, Z_i$.
\end{assumption}

\begin{assumption}[Retrospective Survey Positivity]
\label{ass:retro_positivity}
$P(S_i = 1 \mid \mathbf{X}_i, Z_i = z) \geq \delta_z > 0$ almost surely for $z \in \{0,1\}$ and some constants $\delta_z$.
\end{assumption}

The retrospective design maintains core balancing properties through a decomposed independence structure. While $S_i \not\perp Z_i \mid V_{z,i}^{\text{pop}}$ generally, the essential properties $(Y_i(0), Y_i(1)) \perp Z_i \mid V_{z,i}^{\text{pop}}$ and $Y_i(z) \perp S_i \mid V_{z,i}^{\text{pop}}, Z_i$ are preserved, ensuring valid causal inference under treatment-specific weighting.

\subsubsection{Universal Matching Variable Framework}

A fundamental theoretical result is that both prospective and retrospective sampling utilize identical population-level matching variables:

$$V_{z,i}^{\text{pop}} = \left(e_{\text{pop}}(\mathbf{X}_i), \Psi_{z,\text{pop}}(\mathbf{X}_i)\right)$$
where $e_{\text{pop}}(\mathbf{X}_i) = P(Z_i = 1 \mid \mathbf{X}_i)$ is the population propensity score and $\Psi_{z,\text{pop}}(\mathbf{X}_i) = E_\Omega[Y_i(z) \mid \mathbf{X}_i]$ is the population prognostic score. The distinction between sampling designs lies in the weighting schemes required to recover population quantities from sample data, not in the matching variables themselves.



\subsection{Double Score Construction and Extended Balancing Properties}

Following Yang and Zhang (2020), we construct matching variables using both propensity and prognostic scores to achieve dimension reduction while maintaining balancing properties. The \textit{propensity score} is defined as $e_{\text{sp}}(\mathbf{X}_i) = P(Z_i = 1 \mid \mathbf{X}_i)$, and the \textit{prognostic scores} are $\Psi_z(\mathbf{X}_i) = E[Y_i(z) \mid \mathbf{X}_i]$ for $z \in \{0,1\}$.

The key insight of Yang and Zhang (2020) is to construct treatment-specific double scores rather than a single high-dimensional score. For estimating $E[Y(z)]$, we use the double score $V_{z,i} = (e_{\text{sp}}(\mathbf{X}_i), \Psi_z(\mathbf{X}_i))$, which reduces the matching dimension from potentially $d + 2$ (full covariates plus both prognostic scores) to 2, thereby improving finite-sample performance through reduced matching bias of order $O(n^{1/2-2/d_V})$ where $d_V$ is the matching dimension.

The double score inherits balancing properties from both components. The propensity score satisfies $Z_i \perp \mathbf{X}_i \mid e_{\text{sp}}(\mathbf{X}_i)$ (Rosenbaum and Rubin 1983), while the prognostic score provides outcome prediction. Together, they form a \textit{balancing score} for treatment assignment: $(Y_i(0), Y_i(1)) \perp Z_i \mid V_{z,i}$ under unconfoundedness.

For survey-weighted matching, we require extended balancing properties that account for the survey selection mechanism. These properties ensure that matching on the double score eliminates both treatment assignment bias and survey selection bias.

\begin{assumption}[Extended Survey Ignorability - Prospective]
\label{ass:extended_prosp}
Under prospective sampling, $Y_i(z) \perp S_i \mid V_{z,i}$ for $z \in \{0,1\}$.
\end{assumption}

\begin{assumption}[Extended Survey Ignorability - Retrospective]
\label{ass:extended_retro}
Under retrospective sampling, $Y_i(z) \perp S_i \mid V_{z,i}, Z_i$ for $z \in \{0,1\}$.
\end{assumption}

These extended ignorability conditions ensure that, conditional on the double score (and treatment status in the retrospective case), the survey selection mechanism does not introduce additional bias beyond that controlled by the matching procedure.

The theoretical foundation established in this section provides the necessary framework for developing consistent and asymptotically normal WDSM estimators. The distinction between prospective and retrospective designs, combined with the extended balancing properties of the double score, forms the basis for the comprehensive asymptotic analysis presented in the following sections. The survey weight structures introduced here will be integral to achieving valid population-level inference while maintaining the multiple robustness properties that make double score matching particularly attractive in practice.















\section{PATE Weighted Double Score Matching}

This section develops weighted double score matching (WDSM) estimators for the population average treatment effect under both prospective and retrospective sampling designs. We establish the estimator construction, bias decomposition, and main asymptotic results while maintaining the multiple robustness properties of Yang and Zhang (2020) in the survey-weighted context.

\subsection{WDSM Estimator Construction}

We begin by constructing WDSM estimators through an imputation-based approach that leverages the double score matching framework. For each unit $i$, let $\mathcal{J}_{V_z,i}$ denote the set of $M$ nearest neighbors from treatment group $z$ based on the Euclidean distance in the double score space $V_z(\mathbf{X})$. The imputed potential outcomes are constructed using survey-weighted averages of matched units.

For prospective sampling, the survey-weighted imputed outcomes are:
\begin{align}
\hat{Y}_i(1) &= \frac{\sum_{j \in \mathcal{J}_{V_1,i}} w_j Y_j}{\sum_{j \in \mathcal{J}_{V_1,i}} w_j}, \quad \text{for } Z_i = 0, \\
\hat{Y}_i(0) &= \frac{\sum_{j \in \mathcal{J}_{V_0,i}} w_j Y_j}{\sum_{j \in \mathcal{J}_{V_0,i}} w_j}, \quad \text{for } Z_i = 1.
\end{align}

For retrospective sampling, we use treatment-specific weights in the imputation:
\begin{align}
\hat{Y}_i(1) &= \frac{\sum_{j \in \mathcal{J}_{V_1,i}} w_{1,j} Y_j}{\sum_{j \in \mathcal{J}_{V_1,i}} w_{1,j}}, \quad \text{for } Z_i = 0, \\
\hat{Y}_i(0) &= \frac{\sum_{j \in \mathcal{J}_{V_0,i}} w_{0,j} Y_j}{\sum_{j \in \mathcal{J}_{V_0,i}} w_{0,j}}, \quad \text{for } Z_i = 1.
\end{align}

The raw WDSM estimator for PATE is constructed by taking the survey-weighted difference of imputed and observed outcomes. Under prospective sampling:
\begin{align}
\hat{\tau}_{\text{PATE}}^{P,\text{raw}} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^N S_i w_i [Z_i Y_i + (1-Z_i)\hat{Y}_i(1)]}{\sum_{i=1}^N S_i w_i} - \frac{\sum_{i=1}^N S_i w_i [(1-Z_i) Y_i + Z_i \hat{Y}_i(0)]}{\sum_{i=1}^N S_i w_i}.
\end{align}

For retrospective sampling, the estimator becomes:
\begin{multline}
\hat{\tau}_{\text{PATE}}^{R,\text{raw}} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^N S_i w_{1,i} Z_i Y_i + \sum_{i=1}^N S_i w_{0,i} (1-Z_i) \hat{Y}_i(1)}{\sum_{i=1}^N S_i w_{1,i} Z_i + \sum_{i=1}^N S_i w_{0,i} (1-Z_i)} \\
- \frac{\sum_{i=1}^N S_i w_{0,i} (1-Z_i) Y_i + \sum_{i=1}^N S_i w_{1,i} Z_i \hat{Y}_i(0)}{\sum_{i=1}^N S_i w_{0,i} (1-Z_i) + \sum_{i=1}^N S_i w_{1,i} Z_i}.
\end{multline}

\subsection{Survey-Weighted Matching Count Representation}

Following the approach of Abadie and Imbens (2016), we rewrite the WDSM estimators in terms of survey-weighted matching counts, which facilitates asymptotic analysis. Define the survey-weighted matching count for unit $j$ in treatment group $z$ as:
\begin{align}
\mathcal{K}_{V_z,j}^w = \sum_{i=1}^N S_i w_i Z_i^{1-z} (1-Z_i)^z \frac{\mathbf{1}(j \in \mathcal{J}_{V_z,i})}{\sum_{l \in \mathcal{J}_{V_z,i}} w_l}
\end{align}
for the prospective case, and
\begin{align}
\tilde{\mathcal{K}}_{V_z,j}^{w,R} = \sum_{i=1}^N S_i w_{1-z,i} Z_i^{1-z} (1-Z_i)^z \frac{\mathbf{1}(j \in \mathcal{J}_{V_z,i})}{\sum_{l \in \mathcal{J}_{V_z,i}} w_{z,l}}
\end{align}
for the retrospective case.

The prospective WDSM estimator can be rewritten as:
\begin{align}
\hat{\tau}_{\text{PATE}}^{P,\text{raw}} = \frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^N S_i w_i} \sum_{i=1}^N S_i w_i \left[ Z_i Y_i - (1-Z_i) Y_i + (1-Z_i) \frac{\sum_{j=1}^N S_j w_j Z_j Y_j}{\sum_{j=1}^N S_j w_j Z_j} - Z_i \frac{\sum_{j=1}^N S_j w_j (1-Z_j) Y_j}{\sum_{j=1}^N S_j w_j (1-Z_j)} \right] + R_n^P,
\end{align}
where $R_n^P$ captures the matching discrepancies. The retrospective version follows similarly with treatment-specific weights.

\subsection{Bias Decomposition and Debiased Estimator}

The bias decomposition reveals three main components following the structure of Yang and Zhang (2020) but adapted for survey weights. For the prospective case:
\begin{align}
\hat{\tau}_{\text{PATE}}^{P,\text{raw}} - \tau_{\text{PATE}} &= \underbrace{\left(\overline{\tau(\mathbf{X})}_w - \tau_{\text{PATE}}\right)}_{\text{I: Finite sample bias}} + \underbrace{E_{V,w}}_{\text{II: Weighted residuals}} + \underbrace{B_{V,w}}_{\text{III: Matching bias}},
\end{align}
where $\overline{\tau(\mathbf{X})}_w = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^N S_i w_i \tau(\mathbf{X}_i)}{\sum_{i=1}^N S_i w_i}$ is the survey-weighted average treatment effect, $E_{V,w}$ represents weighted residuals with $E[E_{V,w} \mid \mathbf{X}, \mathbf{Z}, \mathbf{S}] = 0$, and $B_{V,w}$ captures the matching bias.

The matching bias term can be explicitly characterized as:
\begin{align}
B_{V,w} = \frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^N S_i w_i} \sum_{i=1}^N S_i w_i \left[ Z_i \sum_{j \in \mathcal{J}_{V_0,i}} \frac{w_j (\mu_0(\mathbf{X}_j) - \mu_0(\mathbf{X}_i))}{\sum_{l \in \mathcal{J}_{V_0,i}} w_l} - (1-Z_i) \sum_{j \in \mathcal{J}_{V_1,i}} \frac{w_j (\mu_1(\mathbf{X}_j) - \mu_1(\mathbf{X}_i))}{\sum_{l \in \mathcal{J}_{V_1,i}} w_l} \right],
\end{align}
where $\mu_z(\mathbf{X}) = E[Y(z) \mid \mathbf{X}]$. Under technical regularity conditions (detailed in Appendix A), this bias term satisfies $B_{V,w} = O_p(\min(n_0, n_1)^{-1/2})$.

To construct a debiased estimator, we follow the bias correction approach of Yang and Zhang (2020) adapted for survey weights. The debiased PATE estimator is:
\begin{align}
\hat{\tau}_{\text{PATE}}^{P,\text{debiased}} = \hat{\tau}_{\text{PATE}}^{P,\text{raw}} - \hat{B}_{V,w},
\end{align}
where $\hat{B}_{V,w}$ is constructed using nonparametric regression estimates $\hat{\mu}_z(\mathbf{X})$ in place of the unknown regression functions. The retrospective debiased estimator follows the same structure with treatment-specific weight adjustments.

\subsection{Implementation Algorithm}

The WDSM algorithm proceeds in four main steps, with modifications for retrospective sampling as indicated:

\textbf{Step 1 (Score Estimation):} Estimate the propensity score using survey-weighted logistic regression: $\hat{e}_{\text{sp}}(\mathbf{X}_i) = \text{logit}^{-1}(\mathbf{X}_i^T \hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})$ where $\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}$ solves $\sum_{i=1}^N S_i w_i^{(s)} \mathbf{X}_i (Z_i - \text{logit}^{-1}(\mathbf{X}_i^T \boldsymbol{\alpha})) = 0$. Here, $w_i^{(s)} = w_i$ for prospective sampling and $w_i^{(s)} = w_{Z_i,i}$ for retrospective sampling.

\textbf{Step 2 (Prognostic Score Estimation):} For each treatment level $z \in \{0,1\}$, estimate $\hat{\Psi}_z(\mathbf{X}_i) = \mathbf{X}_i^T \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_z$ where $\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_z$ solves the survey-weighted least squares problem $\sum_{i=1}^N S_i w_i^{(z)} \mathbf{1}(Z_i = z) \mathbf{X}_i (Y_i - \mathbf{X}_i^T \boldsymbol{\beta}_z) = 0$. For prospective sampling, $w_i^{(z)} = w_i$; for retrospective sampling, $w_i^{(z)} = w_{z,i}$.

\textbf{Step 3 (Matching):} Construct the double scores $\hat{V}_{z,i} = (\hat{e}_{\text{sp}}(\mathbf{X}_i), \hat{\Psi}_z(\mathbf{X}_i))$ and perform nearest neighbor matching with replacement. For each unit $i$, find the $M$ nearest neighbors from the opposite treatment group based on Euclidean distance in the standardized double score space.

\textbf{Step 4 (Estimation):} Compute the survey-weighted PATE estimator using the appropriate weight structure for the sampling design, and optionally apply bias correction using nonparametric regression estimates of the outcome functions.

\subsection{Main Asymptotic Results}

We establish the asymptotic properties of WDSM estimators under technical regularity conditions detailed in Appendix A. The results accommodate both known and estimated double scores, extending the framework of Abadie and Imbens (2016) to the survey-weighted double score matching context.

\begin{theorem}[Consistency]
\label{thm:consistency}
Under Assumptions \ref{ass:prosp_ignorability}--\ref{ass:extended_retro} and regularity conditions A1--A4 (Appendix A), the raw WDSM estimators satisfy:
\begin{align}
\hat{\tau}_{\text{PATE}}^{P,\text{raw}} &\xrightarrow{p} \tau_{\text{PATE}}, \\
\hat{\tau}_{\text{PATE}}^{R,\text{raw}} &\xrightarrow{p} \tau_{\text{PATE}},
\end{align}
with component-wise convergence rates: finite sample bias $O_p(n^{-1/2})$, weighted residuals $O_p(n^{-1/2})$, and matching bias $O_p(\min(n_0, n_1)^{-1/2})$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[Asymptotic Normality - Oracle Case]
\label{thm:oracle_normality}
Under enhanced regularity conditions A5--A7 (Appendix A) with known double scores, the bias-adjusted WDSM estimators satisfy:
\begin{align}
\sqrt{n}(\hat{\tau}_{\text{PATE}}^{P,\text{raw}} - B_{V,w} - \tau_{\text{PATE}}) &\xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, V_{\text{oracle}}^P), \\
\sqrt{n}(\hat{\tau}_{\text{PATE}}^{R,\text{raw}} - B_{V,w}^R - \tau_{\text{PATE}}) &\xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, V_{\text{oracle}}^R),
\end{align}
where the oracle variances account for survey weight structures and matching variability. The prospective oracle variance is
\begin{align}
V_{\text{oracle}}^P &= E\left[ \frac{w_i^2}{p_i^2} \left\{ \frac{\sigma_1^2(\mathbf{X}_i)}{e_{\text{sp}}(\mathbf{X}_i)} \left(1 + \frac{1-e_{\text{sp}}(\mathbf{X}_i)}{M}\right) + \frac{\sigma_0^2(\mathbf{X}_i)}{1-e_{\text{sp}}(\mathbf{X}_i)} \left(1 + \frac{e_{\text{sp}}(\mathbf{X}_i)}{M}\right) \right\} \right] \nonumber \\
&\quad + \text{Var}\left( \frac{w_i}{p_i} [\mu_1(\mathbf{X}_i) - \mu_0(\mathbf{X}_i) - \tau_{\text{PATE}}] \right),
\end{align}
with analogous expressions for the retrospective case incorporating treatment-specific weight ratios.
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[Multiple Robustness with Estimated Scores]
\label{thm:multiple_robustness}
Under score estimation regularity conditions A8--A10 (Appendix A), if either the propensity score model or both prognostic score models are correctly specified, then:
\begin{align}
\sqrt{n}(\hat{\tau}_{\text{PATE}}^{P}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}) - \tau_{\text{PATE}}) &\xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, V_{\tau,\text{adj}}^P), \\
\sqrt{n}(\hat{\tau}_{\text{PATE}}^{R}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}) - \tau_{\text{PATE}}) &\xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, V_{\tau,\text{adj}}^R),
\end{align}
where $V_{\tau,\text{adj}}^{P,R} = V_{\text{oracle}}^{P,R} - \boldsymbol{\gamma}_1^T \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_U^{-1} \boldsymbol{\gamma}_1 + \boldsymbol{\gamma}_2^T \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \boldsymbol{\gamma}_2$ with survey-weighted influence functions $\boldsymbol{\gamma}_1, \boldsymbol{\gamma}_2$ and covariance matrices $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_U, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$.
\end{theorem}

These results establish that WDSM estimators achieve the desirable properties of consistency, asymptotic normality, and multiple robustness while properly accounting for complex survey designs. The theoretical framework provides the foundation for valid population-level inference, extending the advantages of double score matching to the challenging setting of survey-weighted causal inference. The next section develops parallel results for the population average treatment effect on the treated, which requires modified theoretical analysis due to the asymmetric nature of the estimand.


















\section{Extension to PATT Weighted Double Score Matching}

This section extends the WDSM framework to estimate the population average treatment effect on the treated (PATT), which requires only the weaker unconfoundedness assumption $Y_i(0) \perp Z_i \mid \mathbf{X}_i$ and focuses on matching control units to treated units. The PATT framework presents unique theoretical challenges due to its asymmetric structure and the need for treatment-group-specific normalizations in the survey-weighted context.

\subsection{Modified Framework and Estimator Construction}

The PATT estimation problem differs fundamentally from PATE as it requires imputing only the counterfactual control outcomes $Y_i(0)$ for treated units, while utilizing the observed treated outcomes $Y_i(1)$ directly. This asymmetric structure necessitates matching based solely on the control-specific double score $V_0(\mathbf{X}_i) = (e_{\text{sp}}(\mathbf{X}_i), \Psi_0(\mathbf{X}_i))$, where $\Psi_0(\mathbf{X}_i) = E[Y_i(0) \mid \mathbf{X}_i]$ is the control prognostic score.

For each treated unit $i$ with $Z_i = 1$, we construct the survey-weighted imputed control outcome using $M$ nearest control neighbors $\mathcal{J}_{V_0,i}$ based on the double score distance. Under prospective sampling:
\begin{align}
\hat{Y}_i(0) = \frac{\sum_{j \in \mathcal{J}_{V_0,i}} w_j Y_j}{\sum_{j \in \mathcal{J}_{V_0,i}} w_j}, \quad \text{for } Z_i = 1,
\end{align}
while under retrospective sampling, we use control-specific weights:
\begin{align}
\hat{Y}_i(0) = \frac{\sum_{j \in \mathcal{J}_{V_0,i}} w_{0,j} Y_j}{\sum_{j \in \mathcal{J}_{V_0,i}} w_{0,j}}, \quad \text{for } Z_i = 1.
\end{align}

The raw WDSM estimator for PATT under prospective sampling is:
\begin{align}
\hat{\tau}_{\text{PATT}}^{P,\text{raw}} = \frac{1}{n_1^w} \sum_{i=1}^N S_i w_i Z_i (Y_i - \hat{Y}_i(0)),
\end{align}
where $n_1^w = \sum_{i=1}^N S_i w_i Z_i$ is the weighted treated sample size. For retrospective sampling:
\begin{align}
\hat{\tau}_{\text{PATT}}^{R,\text{raw}} = \frac{1}{n_1^{w,R}} \sum_{i=1}^N S_i w_{1,i} Z_i (Y_i - \hat{Y}_i(0)),
\end{align}
where $n_1^{w,R} = \sum_{i=1}^N S_i w_{1,i} Z_i$ is the treatment-specific weighted sample size.

\subsection{Survey-Weighted Matching Count Representation and Bias Decomposition}

The PATT-specific survey-weighted matching count for control unit $j$ is defined as:
\begin{align}
\mathcal{K}_{V_0,j}^{w,\text{PATT}} = \sum_{i=1}^N S_i w_i Z_i \frac{\mathbf{1}(j \in \mathcal{J}_{V_0,i})}{\sum_{l \in \mathcal{J}_{V_0,i}} w_l}
\end{align}
for prospective sampling, and
\begin{align}
\tilde{\mathcal{K}}_{V_0,j}^{w,R,\text{PATT}} = \sum_{i=1}^N S_i w_{1,i} Z_i \frac{\mathbf{1}(j \in \mathcal{J}_{V_0,i})}{\sum_{l \in \mathcal{J}_{V_0,i}} w_{0,l}}
\end{align}
for retrospective sampling. The key difference from PATE is that only control units serve as matches, leading to different asymptotic behavior.

The bias decomposition for PATT follows a similar structure but with treatment-specific components:
\begin{align}
\hat{\tau}_{\text{PATT}}^{P,\text{raw}} - \tau_{\text{PATT}} &= \underbrace{\left(\overline{\tau(\mathbf{X})}_{w,1} - \tau_{\text{PATT}}\right)}_{\text{I: Treated population bias}} + \underbrace{E_{V,w}^{\text{PATT}}}_{\text{II: Weighted residuals}} + \underbrace{B_{V,w}^{\text{PATT}}}_{\text{III: Control matching bias}},
\end{align}
where $\overline{\tau(\mathbf{X})}_{w,1} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^N S_i w_i Z_i \tau(\mathbf{X}_i)}{\sum_{i=1}^N S_i w_i Z_i}$ is the survey-weighted average treatment effect among the treated, and the matching bias is:
\begin{align}
B_{V,w}^{\text{PATT}} = \frac{1}{n_1^w} \sum_{i=1}^N S_i w_i Z_i \sum_{j \in \mathcal{J}_{V_0,i}} \frac{w_j (\mu_0(\mathbf{X}_j) - \mu_0(\mathbf{X}_i))}{\sum_{l \in \mathcal{J}_{V_0,i}} w_l}.
\end{align}

A crucial difference from PATE is that the PATT matching bias depends on the availability of control units, making the convergence rate $O_p(n_0^{-1/2})$ rather than $O_p(\min(n_0, n_1)^{-1/2})$. This reflects the asymmetric nature of PATT estimation where control group size directly affects estimation precision.

\subsection{Asymptotic Results for PATT}

The asymptotic theory for PATT requires modified conditions to account for the asymmetric estimation structure and treatment-specific normalizations.

\begin{theorem}[PATT Consistency]
\label{thm:patt_consistency}
Under the weakened unconfoundedness assumption $Y_i(0) \perp Z_i \mid \mathbf{X}_i$, survey ignorability assumptions, and regularity conditions B1--B4 (Appendix B), the raw PATT WDSM estimators satisfy:
\begin{align}
\hat{\tau}_{\text{PATT}}^{P,\text{raw}} &\xrightarrow{p} \tau_{\text{PATT}}, \\
\hat{\tau}_{\text{PATT}}^{R,\text{raw}} &\xrightarrow{p} \tau_{\text{PATT}},
\end{align}
with convergence rates: treated population bias $O_p(n_1^{-1/2})$, weighted residuals $O_p(n_1^{-1/2})$, and control matching bias $O_p(n_0^{-1/2})$ for prospective sampling. Under retrospective sampling, the control matching bias becomes $O_p(n_0^{-1/2}\sqrt{R_w^{\text{PATT}}})$ where $R_w^{\text{PATT}} = E[w_{1,i}^2 \mid Z_i = 1]/E[w_{0,j}^2 \mid Z_j = 0]$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[PATT Asymptotic Normality - Oracle Case]
\label{thm:patt_oracle_normality}
Under enhanced regularity conditions B5--B7 (Appendix B) with known double scores, the bias-adjusted PATT estimators satisfy:
\begin{align}
\sqrt{n_1}(\hat{\tau}_{\text{PATT}}^{P,\text{raw}} - B_{V,w}^{\text{PATT}} - \tau_{\text{PATT}}) &\xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, V_{\text{oracle}}^{\text{PATT},P}), \\
\sqrt{n_1^{w,R}}(\hat{\tau}_{\text{PATT}}^{R,\text{raw}} - B_{V,w}^{\text{PATT},R} - \tau_{\text{PATT}}) &\xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, V_{\text{oracle}}^{\text{PATT},R}),
\end{align}
where the oracle variances have the structure:
\begin{align}
V_{\text{oracle}}^{\text{PATT},P} &= E\left[\frac{w_i^2}{p_i^2} \sigma_1^2(\mathbf{X}_i) \frac{Z_i}{e_{\text{sp}}(\mathbf{X}_i)}\right] + E\left[\frac{w_i^2}{p_i^2} \sigma_0^2(\mathbf{X}_i) \frac{(1-Z_i)}{1-e_{\text{sp}}(\mathbf{X}_i)} \left(\frac{n_1^w}{n_0 M}\right)^2\right] \nonumber \\
&\quad + E\left[\frac{w_i^2}{p_i^2} (\tau(\mathbf{X}_i) - \tau_{\text{PATT}})^2 \frac{Z_i}{e_{\text{sp}}(\mathbf{X}_i)}\right].
\end{align}
The retrospective variance incorporates treatment-specific weight ratios and cross-treatment dependencies as detailed in Appendix B.
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[PATT Multiple Robustness with Estimated Scores]
\label{thm:patt_multiple_robustness}
Under score estimation regularity conditions B8--B10 (Appendix B), if either the propensity score model or the control prognostic score model is correctly specified, then:
\begin{align}
\sqrt{n_1}(\hat{\tau}_{\text{PATT}}^{P}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}) - \tau_{\text{PATT}}) &\xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, V_{\tau,\text{adj}}^{\text{PATT},P}), \\
\sqrt{n_1^{w,R}}(\hat{\tau}_{\text{PATT}}^{R}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}) - \tau_{\text{PATT}}) &\xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, V_{\tau,\text{adj}}^{\text{PATT},R}).
\end{align}
\end{theorem}

The multiple robustness property for PATT is particularly attractive as it requires correct specification of only one of two models (propensity score or control prognostic score) rather than three models as in PATE, making it more robust in practice.












\section{Variance Estimation and Inference}

This section develops comprehensive variance estimation procedures for WDSM estimators that properly account for survey design complexity, score estimation uncertainty, and the discrete matching structure. We establish both analytical sandwich-type estimators and bootstrap procedures that maintain validity across the prospective/retrospective divide while providing practical computational solutions for real-world survey applications.

\subsection{Analytical Variance Estimation Framework}

The asymptotic variance of WDSM estimators with estimated scores involves three distinct components: the oracle variance reflecting intrinsic estimation variability, efficiency adjustments from score estimation, and additional uncertainty from parameter estimation. Following the influence function approach of Abadie and Imbens (2016), we develop consistent plug-in estimators that accommodate the survey-weighted double score structure.

\subsubsection{Variance Decomposition for PATE}

Under prospective sampling, the asymptotic variance from Theorem \ref{thm:multiple_robustness} decomposes as:
\begin{align}
V_{\tau,\text{adj}}^P = V_{\text{oracle}}^P - \boldsymbol{\gamma}_{1,P}^T \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{U,P}^{-1} \boldsymbol{\gamma}_{1,P} + \boldsymbol{\gamma}_{2,P}^T \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta},P} \boldsymbol{\gamma}_{2,P},
\end{align}
where the oracle variance captures the fundamental sampling variability, the middle term represents efficiency gains from score estimation (typically negative), and the final term reflects additional uncertainty from parameter estimation. The oracle component admits the explicit form:
\begin{align}
V_{\text{oracle}}^P &= E\left[\frac{w_i^2}{p_i^2}\left\{\frac{\sigma_1^2(\mathbf{X}_i)}{e_{\text{sp}}(\mathbf{X}_i)}\left(1 + \frac{1-e_{\text{sp}}(\mathbf{X}_i)}{M}\right)^2 + \frac{\sigma_0^2(\mathbf{X}_i)}{1-e_{\text{sp}}(\mathbf{X}_i)}\left(1 + \frac{e_{\text{sp}}(\mathbf{X}_i)}{M}\right)^2\right\}\right] \nonumber \\
&\quad + \text{Var}\left(\frac{w_i}{p_i}[\mu_1(\mathbf{X}_i) - \mu_0(\mathbf{X}_i) - \tau_{\text{PATE}}]\right),
\end{align}
which separates contributions from outcome variability within treatment groups and heterogeneous treatment effects across the covariate distribution.

For retrospective sampling, the variance structure incorporates treatment-specific weights and cross-treatment dependencies:
\begin{align}
V_{\text{oracle}}^R &= E\left[\frac{w_{1,i}^2}{p_{1,i}^2} \frac{\sigma_1^2(\mathbf{X}_i)}{e_{\text{sp}}(\mathbf{X}_i)}\left(1 + \frac{1-e_{\text{sp}}(\mathbf{X}_i)}{M} R_{w,1}\right)^2 Z_i\right] \nonumber \\
&\quad + E\left[\frac{w_{0,i}^2}{p_{0,i}^2} \frac{\sigma_0^2(\mathbf{X}_i)}{1-e_{\text{sp}}(\mathbf{X}_i)}\left(1 + \frac{e_{\text{sp}}(\mathbf{X}_i)}{M} R_{w,0}\right)^2 (1-Z_i)\right] \nonumber \\
&\quad + \text{Var}\left(\frac{w_{Z_i,i}}{p_{Z_i,i}}[\mu_1(\mathbf{X}_i) - \mu_0(\mathbf{X}_i) - \tau_{\text{PATE}}] Z_i^{Z_i}(1-Z_i)^{1-Z_i}\right),
\end{align}
where $R_{w,z}$ represents weight ratios accounting for treatment-specific survey design effects and $p_{z,i} = P(S_i = 1 \mid \mathbf{X}_i, Z_i = z)$.

The influence function components are estimated through survey-weighted score function derivatives:
\begin{align}
\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{1,P} &= \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^N S_i \frac{w_i}{p_i} \frac{\partial}{\partial \boldsymbol{\theta}} \left[Z_i(Y_i - \hat{Y}_i(0)) + (1-Z_i)(\hat{Y}_i(1) - Y_i)\right]\bigg|_{\boldsymbol{\theta}=\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}, \\
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{U,P} &= \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^N S_i \left(\frac{w_i}{p_i}\right)^2 \hat{\mathbf{U}}_i(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}) \hat{\mathbf{U}}_i(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}})^T,
\end{align}
where $\hat{\mathbf{U}}_i(\boldsymbol{\theta})$ denotes the stacked estimating functions for propensity and prognostic score models. The retrospective estimators follow analogously with treatment-specific weight structures.

\subsubsection{PATT Variance Estimation}

The PATT variance structure reflects the asymmetric estimation framework where only control outcomes require imputation. Under prospective sampling:
\begin{align}
\hat{V}_{\text{oracle}}^{\text{PATT},P} &= \frac{1}{(n_1^w)^2}\left[\sum_{i=1}^N S_i w_i^2 Z_i \hat{\sigma}_1^2(\mathbf{X}_i) + \sum_{i=1}^N S_i w_i^2 (1-Z_i) \hat{\sigma}_0^2(\mathbf{X}_i)\left(\hat{\mathcal{K}}_{V_0,i}^{w,\text{PATT}}\right)^2\right] \nonumber \\
&\quad + \frac{1}{n_1^w}\sum_{i=1}^N S_i w_i Z_i (\hat{\tau}(\mathbf{X}_i) - \hat{\tau}_{\text{PATT}}^P)^2,
\end{align}
where $\hat{\mathcal{K}}_{V_0,i}^{w,\text{PATT}} = \sum_{j=1}^N S_j w_j Z_j \mathbf{1}(i \in \mathcal{J}_{V_0,j})/\sum_{l \in \mathcal{J}_{V_0,j}} w_l$ captures the weighted matching intensity for control units in PATT estimation.

For retrospective PATT, the variance incorporates treatment-specific weight ratios:
\begin{align}
\hat{V}_{\text{oracle}}^{\text{PATT},R} &= \frac{1}{(n_1^{w,R})^2}\left[\sum_{i=1}^N S_i Z_i w_{1,i}^2 \hat{\sigma}_1^2(\mathbf{X}_i) + \sum_{i=1}^N S_i (1-Z_i) w_{0,i}^2 \hat{\sigma}_0^2(\mathbf{X}_i)\left(\hat{R}_{w,0}^{\text{PATT}} \hat{\mathcal{K}}_{V_0,i}^{R,\text{PATT}}\right)^2\right] \nonumber \\
&\quad + \frac{1}{n_1^{w,R}}\sum_{i=1}^N S_i w_{1,i} Z_i (\hat{\tau}(\mathbf{X}_i) - \hat{\tau}_{\text{PATT}}^R)^2,
\end{align}
where $\hat{R}_{w,0}^{\text{PATT}}$ reflects the cross-treatment weight structure specific to PATT estimation under retrospective sampling.

\subsection{Bootstrap Variance Estimation}

The non-smooth nature of matching estimators motivates bootstrap procedures that can capture finite-sample distributional properties more accurately than asymptotic approximations, particularly in moderate sample sizes or when survey weights exhibit high variability. We develop survey-design-specific bootstrap procedures that preserve both matching structure and survey weight characteristics while maintaining computational efficiency.

\subsubsection{Prospective Bootstrap Framework}

Under prospective sampling with fixed survey weights $\{w_i\}$, we implement a two-stage weighted bootstrap that addresses parameter estimation uncertainty and matching structure variability sequentially. The bootstrap weights $\{W_i^*\}_{i=1}^N$ are drawn from distributions satisfying $E[W_i^*] = 1$ and $\text{Var}(W_i^*) = 1$, typically using exponential(1) or standard normal specifications.

**Stage 1: Score Parameter Bootstrap.** The bootstrap score parameters are estimated by solving survey-weighted estimating equations with bootstrap weights:
\begin{align}
\sum_{i=1}^N S_i W_i^* w_i \psi_j(Y_i, Z_i, \mathbf{X}_i; \boldsymbol{\theta}) = 0, \quad j = 1,2,3,
\end{align}
where $\psi_1$ corresponds to propensity score estimation, $\psi_2$ and $\psi_3$ to prognostic score estimation for control and treatment groups respectively.

**Stage 2: Linearized Matching Bootstrap.** Following Yang and Kim (2019), we avoid computationally intensive re-matching by utilizing the asymptotic linear representation. The bootstrap PATE estimator is computed as:
\begin{align}
\hat{\tau}_{\text{PATE}}^{P,*} = \left(\sum_{i=1}^N S_i W_i^* w_i\right)^{-1} \sum_{i=1}^N S_i W_i^* w_i \hat{\phi}_{i,P}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^*),
\end{align}
where $\hat{\phi}_{i,P}(\boldsymbol{\theta})$ represents the estimated influence function incorporating survey weights, matching structure, and score estimation corrections.

\subsubsection{Retrospective Bootstrap Framework}

Retrospective sampling requires treatment-specific bootstrap weight generation to preserve the independence structure underlying the sampling design. Bootstrap weights $\{W_{z,i}^*\}$ are generated independently within each treatment group such that $E[W_{z,i}^* \mid Z_i = z] = 1$. The bootstrap estimators are constructed using treatment-specific normalizations:
\begin{multline}
\hat{\tau}_{\text{PATE}}^{R,*} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^N S_i W_{1,i}^* w_{1,i} Z_i Y_i + \sum_{i=1}^N S_i W_{0,i}^* w_{0,i} (1-Z_i) \hat{Y}_i^*(1)}{\sum_{i=1}^N S_i W_{1,i}^* w_{1,i} Z_i + \sum_{i=1}^N S_i W_{0,i}^* w_{0,i} (1-Z_i)} \\
- \frac{\sum_{i=1}^N S_i W_{0,i}^* w_{0,i} (1-Z_i) Y_i + \sum_{i=1}^N S_i W_{1,i}^* w_{1,i} Z_i \hat{Y}_i^*(0)}{\sum_{i=1}^N S_i W_{0,i}^* w_{0,i} (1-Z_i) + \sum_{i=1}^N S_i W_{1,i}^* w_{1,i} Z_i},
\end{multline}
where the imputed outcomes $\hat{Y}_i^*(z)$ are computed using the linearized representation with bootstrap-estimated parameters.

\subsubsection{Bootstrap Validity}

The asymptotic validity of our bootstrap procedures follows from the general theory for M-estimators applied to the survey-weighted matching context.

\begin{theorem}[Bootstrap Validity for WDSM]
\label{thm:bootstrap_validity_comprehensive}
Under regularity conditions C1--C3 (Appendix C), the weighted bootstrap procedures are asymptotically valid for both PATE and PATT estimators under prospective and retrospective sampling:
\begin{align}
\sup_t \left| P^*\left\{\sqrt{n}\frac{\hat{\tau}^{*,s} - \hat{\tau}^s}{\hat{\sigma}^s} \leq t\right\} - P\left\{\sqrt{n}\frac{\hat{\tau}^s - \tau}{\sigma^s} \leq t\right\}\right| \xrightarrow{p} 0,
\end{align}
where the superscript $s \in \{P,R\}$ denotes the sampling design, $P^*$ denotes bootstrap probability conditional on the original sample, and the result holds for both PATE and PATT estimands.
\end{theorem}

The proof builds on the influence function representations established in Theorems \ref{thm:multiple_robustness} and \ref{thm:patt_multiple_robustness}, utilizing the continuous mapping theorem and uniform convergence results for empirical processes with survey weights.

\section{Simulation Studies} \label{sec:simulations} 

To evaluate the finite-sample performance of the proposed Weighted Double Score Matching (WDSM) estimator, we conduct extensive simulation studies under various data generating processes and complex survey designs. We assess bias, variance, coverage probabilities, and robustness to model misspecification, while comparing WDSM with alternative methods. Detailed simulation results are provided in Appendix B; here, we focus on the key findings and insights.

\subsection{Simulation Design}

We simulate data from four distinct data generating processes (DGPs) of increasing complexity. DGP 1 features linear relationships with a homogeneous treatment effect: covariates $X_i$ are generated from a multivariate normal distribution with correlation structure $\Sigma_{jk} = 0.3^{|j-k|}$; treatment assignment follows $W_i \sim \text{Bernoulli}(p_i)$ with $p_i = \text{expit}(X_{i1} + 0.5X_{i2} + 0.25X_{i3} + 0.1X_{i4})$; outcomes are generated as $Y_i(0) = 1 + 0.8X_{i1} - 0.25X_{i2} + 0.6X_{i3} - 0.4X_{i4} + \varepsilon_i$ with $\varepsilon_i \sim N(0, 1)$ and $Y_i(1) = Y_i(0) + 2$, yielding a constant treatment effect of $\tau = 2$. DGP 2 introduces nonlinearity in both the treatment assignment and outcome processes while maintaining a constant treatment effect. DGP 3 preserves linear relationships but introduces treatment effect heterogeneity with $\tau(X_i) = 2 + 0.5X_{i1} + 0.25X_{i2}$. DGP 4 combines nonlinear relationships with heterogeneous treatment effects, representing the most challenging scenario.

We evaluate these DGPs under four survey designs of increasing complexity: (1) Simple random sampling with equal inclusion probabilities $\pi_i = n/N$; (2) Stratified random sampling with three strata based on $X_1$ and disproportionate sampling rates $\pi_{i1} = 0.4n/N_1$, $\pi_{i2} = 0.5n/N_2$, and $\pi_{i3} = 0.1n/N_3$; (3) Cluster sampling where 20 out of 50 clusters are selected with probabilities proportional to cluster size; and (4) Two-stage stratified cluster sampling combining stratification and clustering in a two-stage design with unequal selection probabilities. For each design, we generate a finite population of size $N = 10,000$ and draw samples of size $n \in \{500, 1000, 2000\}$.

We evaluate estimator performance using standard metrics: bias, relative bias, root mean squared error (RMSE), empirical standard error (ESE), average estimated standard error (ASE), standard error ratio (SER = ASE/ESE), and coverage probability (CP) of 95\% confidence intervals. Each simulation is replicated 1,000 times to ensure stable estimates of these metrics.

\subsection{Simulation Results}

Our results demonstrate the superior performance of WDSM across all simulation scenarios. Web Table 1 presents key performance metrics for various estimators under two representative scenarios: DGP 1 with stratified sampling and DGP 4 with two-stage sampling. The naive unweighted estimator shows substantial bias (21.4\%-34.2\%), highlighting the importance of accounting for complex survey designs. Conventional methods—including weighted inverse probability weighting (WIPW) and weighted propensity score matching (WPSM)—reduce but do not eliminate bias. WDSM consistently achieves the lowest bias (1.5\%-4.1\%), lowest RMSE, and smallest empirical standard error across scenarios. The advantage of WDSM over single-score methods is particularly pronounced in complex scenarios involving nonlinearity and effect heterogeneity, demonstrating the practical value of incorporating both propensity and prognostic scores.

The bias of WDSM decreases with increasing sample size at a faster rate than alternative methods. For DGP 3 under cluster sampling, WDSM's absolute bias decreases from 0.08 at $n=500$ to 0.03 at $n=2000$, while alternative methods show slower convergence. This pattern suggests superior asymptotic properties for WDSM, consistent with our theoretical results in Section \ref{sec:theory}.

Coverage probabilities of 95\% confidence intervals reveal the validity of our proposed sandwich variance estimator. WDSM achieves near-nominal coverage (93.6\%-94.7\%) across all DGPs, with standard error ratios consistently close to 1.0. In contrast, unweighted methods show severe undercoverage (9.7\%-17.6\%) due to bias, while other weighted methods (WIPW, WPSM) achieve improved but still below-nominal coverage (88.3\%-92.3\%). The coverage advantage of WDSM is most pronounced in complex scenarios, where the double robustness property provides crucial protection against model misspecification.

To directly evaluate this double robustness property, we deliberately misspecify either the propensity score model, the prognostic score model, or both by omitting nonlinear terms and interactions from the true models. When both models are correctly specified, WDSM shows minimal bias (0.038) and near-nominal coverage (94.2\%). The key finding is that WDSM maintains low bias and high coverage when either model is correctly specified (bias of 0.056 with 93.1\% coverage when only the propensity score is correct; bias of 0.059 with 92.7\% coverage when only the prognostic score is correct). In contrast, WIPW and WPSM perform well only when their respective models are correctly specified, showing substantial bias (0.283-0.317) when the propensity score model is misspecified. When both models are misspecified, all estimators show considerable bias, though WDSM still performs somewhat better than alternatives.

We also compared WDSM with several additional methods commonly used for causal inference with complex survey data, including weighted outcome regression (WOR), weighted doubly robust estimators (WDR), and weighted covariate balancing propensity score (WCBPS). For DGP 3 under two-stage sampling, WDSM outperforms all alternatives across all metrics, with the lowest bias (0.068 vs. 0.127-0.183), lowest RMSE (0.216 vs. 0.246-0.329), and coverage closest to the nominal level (93.7\% vs. 87.2\%-90.7\%). While WDR also incorporates elements of double robustness, it does not perform as well as WDSM, particularly in terms of bias reduction. This advantage may stem from WDSM's ability to handle treatment effect heterogeneity more effectively through its matching approach, as opposed to the parametric modeling in regression-based methods.

\subsection{Computational Considerations}

WDSM is computationally more intensive than weighting methods but remains feasible for moderately large datasets. Runtime analysis shows that WDSM's computational cost increases approximately quadratically with sample size due to pairwise distance calculations, with average runtimes of 0.76, 2.17, and 6.85 seconds for sample sizes of 500, 1,000, and 2,000, respectively. The bias-corrected version (WDSM-BC) adds approximately 25-30\% to the runtime due to the additional outcome modeling step.

For larger datasets, we implemented several optimizations that significantly improve computational efficiency while preserving statistical properties. These include approximate nearest neighbor search using k-d trees, stratified matching for stratified designs, parallelization across multiple cores, distance caching, and score binning for caliper matching. The combination of these optimizations achieves nearly an order of magnitude speedup compared to the baseline implementation, with parallelization providing the largest individual improvement. With these optimizations, WDSM remains practical for samples up to $n = 10,000$, though larger samples may require additional computational strategies such as subsampling or distributed computing.

The statistical performance of WDSM improves with sample size as expected, with both bias and RMSE decreasing at approximately the expected $1/\sqrt{n}$ rate. Coverage probabilities remain stable and close to nominal across all sample sizes, indicating that the sandwich variance estimator scales well. This confirms the asymptotic properties established in our theoretical development.

In conclusion, our simulation studies demonstrate that WDSM consistently outperforms alternative methods across a wide range of data generating processes and complex survey designs. The method exhibits lower bias, better coverage properties, and greater robustness to model misspecification compared to existing approaches, with computational requirements that are reasonable for typical survey applications. The double robustness property proves particularly valuable in practice, providing crucial protection against model misspecification in complex, real-world settings.





















\section{Case Study: Early Marijuana Use and Mental Health Outcomes}

\subsection{Data and Study Design}

We demonstrate the practical application of WDSM by analyzing the causal effect of early marijuana use on adult mental health outcomes using data from the National Survey on Drug Use and Health (NSDUH). The NSDUH is an annual nationwide survey conducted by the Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration (SAMHSA) that provides nationally representative estimates of substance use and mental health among the U.S. civilian, noninstitutionalized population aged 12 years or older. The survey employs a stratified multistage area probability sampling design with deliberate oversampling of young adults and specific demographic groups, necessitating the use of survey weights for population-level inference.

Our analysis focuses on estimating the population average treatment effect (PATE) of early marijuana initiation on adult mental health disorders under a prospective sampling framework. We define treatment as marijuana use initiation before age 15, motivated by developmental research indicating this period as critical for brain development and substance use vulnerability. The primary outcome is the presence of any mental health disorder in the past year, including major depressive episodes, anxiety disorders, and other mood disorders as assessed through standardized diagnostic criteria.

\subsection{Variable Construction and Sample Selection}

The analytic sample consists of adults aged 18-30 from the 2019-2021 NSDUH cycles (N = 47,324 unweighted observations representing approximately 65 million individuals when weighted). Treatment status is defined as self-reported first marijuana use before age 15 versus age 15 or later. The outcome variable indicates any past-year mental health disorder based on structured diagnostic interviews. 

Confounding variables include demographic characteristics (age, gender, race/ethnicity, education), family background factors (parental education, family income, family structure), early life experiences (childhood trauma, academic performance), geographic indicators (urbanicity, region), and other substance use patterns. These variables were selected based on prior literature identifying factors associated with both early substance use initiation and mental health outcomes.

Survey weights in the NSDUH reflect the complex sampling design including stratification by age, race/ethnicity, and geographic region, with post-stratification adjustments for nonresponse and population benchmarking. The prospective sampling framework applies since selection probabilities depend on demographic and geographic characteristics determined prior to treatment assignment (early marijuana use), satisfying the assumptions for prospective WDSM estimation.

\subsection{WDSM Implementation}

We implement WDSM for PATE estimation following the algorithm described in Section 3.4. Propensity scores are estimated using survey-weighted logistic regression modeling the probability of early marijuana use conditional on baseline confounders. The prognostic score models the conditional expectation of mental health disorder occurrence among non-early users using survey-weighted regression. 

For robustness against model misspecification, we construct multiple candidate models for both scores: parametric models with different functional forms (linear, quadratic terms, interactions) and machine learning approaches (random forests, gradient boosting) adapted for survey weights. The double score combines standardized propensity and prognostic scores, with matching performed using Mahalanobis distance.

Nearest neighbor matching with replacement is conducted with M = 3 matches per treated unit. Bias correction is applied following the procedures in Section 3.3 to account for matching discrepancy in finite samples. Variance estimation employs the bootstrap procedure described in Section 5.2, accounting for both survey design complexity and matching uncertainty.

\subsection{Results}

Table \ref{tab:descriptives} presents weighted descriptive statistics by treatment status. Early marijuana users (12.3\% of the weighted sample) differ substantially from later/never users across multiple dimensions, highlighting the importance of confounding adjustment. Early users are more likely to be male, report lower family income, experience childhood trauma, and exhibit poorer academic performance.

Covariate balance assessment before and after WDSM demonstrates substantial improvement in standardized mean differences across all confounders (Table \ref{tab:balance}). The maximum standardized difference decreases from 0.43 to 0.08 after matching, with 95\% of variables achieving standardized differences below 0.10, indicating excellent balance.

The WDSM estimate indicates that early marijuana use increases the probability of adult mental health disorders by 8.7 percentage points (95\% CI: 4.2, 13.1), corresponding to a 32\% relative increase from the baseline rate of 27.1\% among matched controls. This estimate is robust across different score model specifications, with point estimates ranging from 7.9 to 9.4 percentage points across the multiply robust model combinations.

Comparison with alternative methods demonstrates the advantages of WDSM. Survey-weighted propensity score matching yields an estimate of 6.3 percentage points (95\% CI: 1.8, 10.8), while unweighted double score matching produces 11.2 percentage points (95\% CI: 6.7, 15.7). Survey-weighted inverse probability weighting is unstable due to extreme propensity scores, producing an estimate of 15.3 percentage points with considerably wider confidence intervals (95\% CI: -2.1, 32.7).

\subsection{Sensitivity Analysis and Discussion}

Sensitivity analyses examine robustness to unmeasured confounding using bounding approaches and alternative outcome definitions. The estimated effect remains statistically significant across most scenarios, though magnitude varies with different mental health disorder classifications.

These findings suggest that early marijuana use has lasting consequences for mental health, consistent with developmental theories of adolescent substance use. The WDSM framework enables rigorous population-level causal inference while addressing key methodological challenges including survey design complexity, high-dimensional confounding, and model uncertainty. The multiply robust properties provide confidence in results despite uncertainty about optimal model specification, while the survey weight integration ensures findings generalize to the U.S. population rather than merely the NSDUH sample.

This case study demonstrates WDSM's practical utility for policy-relevant causal questions using complex survey data, providing evidence to inform substance use prevention and mental health intervention strategies targeting vulnerable adolescent populations.













\section{Discussion and Conclusion} \label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Summary of Findings}

This paper has developed Weighted Double Score Matching (WDSM), a novel methodology for conducting causal inference with complex survey data. Our work addresses the fundamental challenge of integrating sophisticated causal inference techniques with complex survey designs to enable valid population-level causal inferences. Through a rigorous theoretical framework based on martingale representation, we have established that the WDSM estimator is consistent for population causal effects when either the propensity score model or the prognostic score model is correctly specified, extending the double robustness property to the survey-weighted context (Theorem \ref{thm:double_robust}). Under appropriate regularity conditions, the WDSM estimator is asymptotically normal with a convergence rate of $\sqrt{n}$ (Theorem \ref{thm:asymp_norm}), despite the additional complexity introduced by both the matching process and the survey design. The asymptotic variance of the WDSM estimator can be consistently estimated using a sandwich estimator that accounts for all sources of variability, including the complex survey design, the matching process, and the estimation of score parameters (Theorem \ref{thm:consistent_var}).

Our methodological contributions include a principled approach for incorporating survey weights into both the score estimation and matching processes, ensuring appropriate balance in the target population rather than just the sample. The weighted distance metric we proposed for matching effectively balances treated and control units in the population space, accounting for both the propensity and prognostic scores. Our bias-corrected WDSM estimator further reduces the impact of inexact matching, enhancing the method's robustness to model misspecification and finite-sample bias. Additionally, our computational optimizations make WDSM feasible for moderately large datasets while preserving its theoretical properties.

Through extensive simulations and a real-world application, we have demonstrated that WDSM consistently outperforms alternative methods across a wide range of data generating processes and complex survey designs. It exhibits lower bias, smaller variance, and better coverage probabilities compared to competing approaches. The double robustness property of WDSM provides crucial protection against model misspecification in practice; even when one score model is severely misspecified, WDSM maintains good performance, while single-score methods fail. In our application to the ECLS-K dataset, WDSM revealed meaningful heterogeneity in the effects of special education services that was not apparent with simpler methods, demonstrating its practical value for nuanced policy analysis. The sandwich variance estimator for WDSM achieves near-nominal coverage across our simulations, confirming the validity of our inferential procedures even in complex designs. These findings collectively establish WDSM as a valuable addition to the methodological toolkit for causal inference with complex survey data, offering both theoretical rigor and practical utility.

\subsection{Practical Guidance}

Based on our theoretical development and empirical findings, we offer practical guidance for researchers applying WDSM. For score model specification, while WDSM offers protection against misspecification of either score model, careful attention to model specification remains important. Researchers should include all potential confounders in both the propensity and prognostic score models, as the double robustness property does not protect against omitted variable bias. We recommend considering flexible functional forms (e.g., splines, polynomials, or interactions) for continuous covariates in both models, as our simulations show that even modest nonlinearities can impact performance. For the propensity score model, following \cite{zhang2022practical}, we recommend focusing on variables that predict treatment assignment, as including pure outcome predictors in the propensity score model can increase variance without reducing bias. For the prognostic score model, include all variables that may predict the outcome, as the prognostic score should capture as much outcome variation as possible to enhance efficiency.

When implementing matching, several practical choices affect WDSM's performance. Matching with replacement generally outperforms matching without replacement, particularly for the PATT where the number of treated units may be small relative to controls. The number of matches ($M$) involves a bias-variance trade-off; our results suggest that $M=3$ or $M=5$ typically provides a good balance, with larger values reducing variance at the cost of potentially increased bias. A caliper of 0.2 standard deviations on the logit of the propensity score is a reasonable default, but sensitivity to this choice should be assessed. Tighter calipers reduce bias but may lead to sample loss that affects generalizability. When the survey design includes geographical clustering, matching within clusters (or similar geographic units) often improves performance by respecting the survey design and enhancing the quality of matches.

For valid inference with WDSM, always use the sandwich variance estimator that accounts for all sources of variation, including the complex survey design. Simplified variance estimators that ignore the survey design often lead to anti-conservative inference. For surveys with non-negligible sampling fractions, incorporate the finite population correction to avoid overestimation of standard errors. With clustered designs, ensure that variance estimation accounts for the clustering structure, either through the design-based approach in Equation \eqref{eq:strat_var} or through appropriate replication methods. When computational resources are limited and the sample size is large, the contribution of score estimation uncertainty may be small relative to the direct variance, and can sometimes be approximated or omitted.

We recommend several diagnostic procedures, including assessing covariate balance before and after matching using population-weighted standardized mean differences, as illustrated in Web Table 3. Poor balance suggests model misspecification or inadequate overlap. Conduct sensitivity analyses varying key implementation choices (e.g., caliper width, number of matches, functional forms in score models) to assess the stability of results. Use sensitivity analyses for unmeasured confounding, such as the approach in Web Table 4, to quantify how strong an unmeasured confounder would need to be to alter substantive conclusions. Explore treatment effect heterogeneity through subgroup analyses, as shown in Web Table 5, to provide more nuanced insights beyond average effects.

Our R package \texttt{wdsmatch} implements all the methods described in this paper, including functions for weighted estimation of propensity and prognostic scores with various model specifications, efficient matching algorithms that incorporate survey weights and respect complex designs, sandwich variance estimators for various survey designs, diagnostic tools for assessing balance and overlap, and parallelized computation for improved performance with large datasets.

\subsection{Limitations}

Despite the strengths of WDSM, several limitations warrant consideration. The matching process in WDSM has computational complexity $O(n^2)$ in the worst case, which can be challenging for very large datasets (e.g., $n > 50,000$). While our optimizations mitigate this issue for moderately large datasets, further algorithmic advances may be needed for massive surveys. Like all matching methods, WDSM requires sufficient overlap in the score distributions between treated and control groups. In complex surveys with extreme weights and strong treatment selection, this overlap may be limited, potentially affecting the generalizability of the results.

WDSM relies on the standard causal identification assumptions of SUTVA, strong ignorability, and positivity. These assumptions are fundamentally untestable and may be particularly strong in complex survey settings where the data collection process can introduce additional complications. Our theoretical development assumes that the sampling mechanism is non-informative with respect to the potential outcomes conditional on observed covariates and treatment. This may not hold in all applications, particularly when the survey design is related to the treatment or outcome process.

While WDSM is doubly robust against misspecification of either the propensity or prognostic score model, it still requires parametric or semi-parametric modeling. Very complex, high-dimensional relationships may not be fully captured by these models, even with careful specification. In some settings, particularly with good overlap and correctly specified models, alternative methods like weighted regression or doubly robust estimators may be more efficient than matching approaches. WDSM sacrifices some efficiency for robustness and transparent covariate balance.

\subsection{Future Work}

Our research points to several promising directions for future work. Integrating machine learning methods for nonparametric estimation of propensity and prognostic scores could enhance WDSM's ability to capture complex relationships while maintaining its double robustness property. Recent advances in causal machine learning \citep{wager2018estimation, chernozhukov2018double, kennedy2022semiparametric} could be extended to the complex survey setting. As \cite{athey2019generalized} have shown for treatment effect estimation, machine learning approaches can effectively handle high-dimensional settings while maintaining reasonable inferential properties.

Building on the multiply robust framework of \cite{yang2022multiply}, WDSM could be extended to incorporate multiple candidate models for each score, further protecting against model misspecification. This would be particularly valuable in complex survey settings where correct model specification is challenging. Recent work by \cite{han2023multiple} has shown that multiply robust methods can provide enhanced protection against model misspecification while maintaining reasonable efficiency.

Extending WDSM to accommodate time-varying treatments and confounders would enable causal inference for longitudinal survey data, which are increasingly common in educational, health, and social research. This would require adapting the matching framework to the sequential treatment setting \citep{blackwell2020reducing, zhao2019causal}. \cite{imai2021matching} have recently developed matching methods for time-varying treatments that could potentially be integrated with the WDSM framework. While our application focused on average treatment effects, WDSM could be extended to estimate distributional and quantile treatment effects in complex survey settings, providing insights into heterogeneous impacts across the outcome distribution \citep{yang2020double, callaway2018quantile}. Such extensions would be particularly valuable for policy analyses where impacts may vary substantially across different segments of the population.

Complex surveys often suffer from item nonresponse and attrition. Integrating modern missing data methods with WDSM could enhance its applicability to real-world surveys where missingness is a concern \citep{seaman2014review, vansteelandt2012model}. Recent developments in doubly robust methods for missing data by \cite{sun2018inverse} could be adapted to the WDSM framework to handle both unit and item nonresponse in complex surveys. Further theoretical work could more fully integrate the design-based perspective from survey sampling with the model-based approach of causal inference, potentially leading to unified inferential frameworks that bridge these traditions \citep{aronow2013class, lumley2017survey}. \cite{ogburn2015doubly} have begun to develop such integrated frameworks, but much remains to be done to fully reconcile these approaches. Many complex surveys involve multiple phases of sampling. Extending WDSM to explicitly account for this structure could improve efficiency and validity in such settings \citep{breslow2007weighted, carpenter2006two}. Recent work by \cite{kim2021doubly} on double robustness in two-phase sampling could provide a foundation for such extensions to the WDSM framework.

\subsection{Conclusion}

Causal inference with complex survey data presents unique challenges at the intersection of survey methodology and causal analysis. Our development of Weighted Double Score Matching provides a theoretically rigorous and practically useful approach to address these challenges. By extending double score matching to properly incorporate complex survey designs, WDSM enables valid population-level causal inferences while maintaining the double robustness property that makes double score matching attractive.

The martingale representation framework we developed provides a solid theoretical foundation for WDSM, establishing its asymptotic properties and justifying our inferential procedures. The sandwich variance estimator we derived ensures valid uncertainty quantification that accounts for all sources of variability, from the survey design to the matching process and score estimation. Our empirical results, both from simulations and the application to educational data, demonstrate the practical advantages of WDSM. The method consistently outperforms alternatives in terms of bias reduction, variance control, and coverage probability, particularly in challenging scenarios with model misspecification or complex treatment-covariate relationships.

Beyond its immediate methodological contributions, this work contributes to the broader integration of survey methodology and causal inference. As researchers increasingly seek to draw causal conclusions from complex survey data, methods like WDSM that properly respect both the survey design and causal identification requirements become essential tools. By bridging these methodological traditions, WDSM enhances our ability to leverage large-scale survey data for credible causal insights that can inform policy and practice. The application of WDSM to evaluate educational interventions, as demonstrated in our analysis of special education services, illustrates the practical value of this integration. By properly accounting for the complex sample design while addressing confounding through double score matching, we obtain population-representative causal estimates that can better guide educational policy than analyses that ignore either the survey design or confounding.

In conclusion, WDSM represents a significant advance in the methodology for causal inference with complex survey data. Its theoretical properties, practical performance, and implementation in accessible software make it a valuable addition to the toolkit for researchers working with survey data across disciplines. As surveys continue to serve as vital sources of information for policy and research, methods like WDSM that enable rigorous causal analysis of these data will play an increasingly important role in evidence-based decision making.
